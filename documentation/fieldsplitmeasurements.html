Field-split AMG: measurements behind the defaults

The defaults of the field-split preconditioner (Theory: the field-split AMG preconditioner) – those of the components and of the blockAMG() preset – were chosen by measurement. This page collects those measurements, as the provenance of the defaults; the theory page explains the method without them. Most were taken on two captured models, a gradient-enhanced damage cylinder through its damage onset and a contact anchor model with many multi-point-constraint slaves, offline on captured systems (dumped with dumps, Dumps of the solves) and live.

Many of them were taken before the composition existed, when the method was one monolithic solver; the composition reproduces that solver’s outer iterations, rebuild decisions and solutions, and replaced it.

The full rigid-body near-null-space

See Near-null-space and the Chebyshev smoother.

On real captured systems the full rigid-body basis needed fewer isolated per-field outer iterations than translations alone, and – unlike translations alone – was robust to both thread count and the Chebyshev power-iteration setting. That was on the scalar backend; the block backend’s hierarchy takes the translations only (see backendBlockSize).

The spectral radius estimate of the Chebyshev smoother

See Near-null-space and the Chebyshev smoother.

Why it changed. AMGCL estimated the spectral radius by power iteration from a start vector seeded per OpenMP thread. With a short power iteration, one captured solve needed far more outer iterations at one thread count than at another, nothing else differing, while the true spectral radius barely moved between the solves of that run. A long power iteration removed that sensitivity, but made the estimate the most expensive stage of the setup.

The estimate against a reference eigensolver. On the scaled displacement blocks and the coarse operators of the corner-node interpolation of captured systems, markedly nonsymmetric ones among them, Arnoldi and Lanczos from the same row-seeded start vector agree to about three digits; Arnoldi is kept, since it holds for nonsymmetric matrices. With the default number of steps, its estimate \(|\theta| + r\) lay slightly above the spectral radius on every operator, well within the smoother’s margin; more steps bring it closer, and too few can leave it well below, where the rigid-body coarse level of a small 2D beam of the test suite diverged.

Outer iterations, setup and wall time, on the displacement block alone of the captured systems of The corner-node two-level cycle with scaled weights and a composed coarse solve and of the damage cylinder after its onset, for AMG(), the rigid-body coarse level and the scaled corner-node interpolation, at several thread counts: the default Arnoldi steps needed about the outer iterations of the former long power iteration, for a fraction of its setup and clearly less wall time, and their outer iterations no longer depend on the thread count at all. A start vector seeded per row alone already removes the thread-count dependence of a power iteration. An adaptive number of steps, stopping once the residual norm of the dominant Ritz pair is small against its Ritz value, needed the same outer iterations with more setup on the small coarse AMG levels, so the estimate takes a fixed number of steps. The contact anchor systems the former long power iteration was chosen on were not measured again.

The translations of a block backend

See The near-null-space of a block backend.

Displacement block alone, on captured systems of the damage cylinder: AMGCL’s constant blocks of \(\hat A_{uu}\) leave row residuals far from exact, against near-exact residuals for the translations \(D^{-1} t\).

Full system, offline, on the captured sequences and on isolated systems of both reference models, the configuration with and without the translations as near-null-space (nearNullspace), outer iterations only: clearly fewer on the damage cylinder, both in its onset sequence and on its isolated systems; on the contact model about the same in its sequence, whose hierarchies were rebuilt more often, and fewer on its isolated systems. A control with unit weights needed exactly the iterations of AMGCL’s identity blocks, system by system, on the cylinder’s isolated systems. With "relax": {"scale": true} added, the translations needed the same outer iterations as a hierarchy built on \(A_{uu}\) itself, solve by solve, on both sequences: the aggregates that differ between the two made no measurable difference. The Jacobi-scaled smoother itself gave fewer outer iterations on the cylinder and more on the contact model, where it also triggered more rebuilds; live, it made neither the L-shaped panel nor the damage cylinder faster.

Live, the defaults with and without the translations, from the start of each run or over the same window, judged per simulated time where the two runs took different load paths: on an L-shaped concrete panel (with quadratic and with linear elements, and with adaptive refinement), a notched three-point bending beam and an anchor pry-out, fewer outer iterations, fewer solves stopping at the iteration cap – almost none left on the panel and the beam – and faster runs, with matching load-displacement curves where the runs completed; on the damage cylinder fewer Newton iterations and a faster run. On the contact model fewer Newton iterations but more outer iterations, level within the run-to-run noise. Hence the default. The translations are the near-null-space of the elastic operator, so they help less once softening makes the operator indefinite: on the damage cylinder after its damage onset, the outer iterations per simulated time were the same with and without them, and more solves stopped at the cap with them.

The order of the block Gauss–Seidel sweep

See The block Gauss–Seidel sweep: Multiplicative.

Damage first against displacement first, offline on the captured systems of the damage cylinder and the contact model: identical outer iteration counts, and somewhat more time per outer iteration. On these models the preconditioner’s strength is its per-field AMG cycles, not the coupling.

Gap compensation

See Gap-compensated tolerance: ResidualGapCompensation.

Gap compensation is a live option only on the scaled system. Per increment it is a clear win there: on a live window of a reference model it substantially cut both the number of true-residual restarts and the outer iterations needed, with no cutbacks or material return-mapping failures. But the scaled system, left uncompensated, happens to over-solve every system by a large factor, and the Newton iteration quietly relies on that: converging merely inside the requested tolerance costs Newton extra iterations per increment, which is enough to park the run above the adaptive time stepper’s criticalIter threshold and so stop it from growing the time increment. In the same window the uncompensated path grew its increment and covered more simulated time, while the compensated one never grew. Judged per unit of simulated time rather than per increment, the per-increment saving is largely or wholly given back. Hence this stays opt-in, for a model still run with krylovOnScaledSystem = true; safetyFactor is the knob between the two regimes there – smaller values solve more accurately and behave more like the uncompensated path – and its optimum has not been explored.

See Rigid-body coarse level: TwoLevel(coarseSpace=RigidBodyModesPerAggregate()).

The control needs the iterations of the hierarchy on the translations within two: the gain is the rotations’. On the larger models the setup took up to 1.2 times that of the hierarchy on the translations, and the solve, setup included, 0.61 of its time on the elastic tunnel system and 0.84 to 0.95 on the others. In a simulation, the testfile deck run with verbosity=info shows the same: each linear solve reaches its tolerance in some 20 outer iterations, and Newton converges in two; with the coarse level switched off, every solve stops at the iteration cap with a relative residual of about 1e-2, and Newton needs five.

Live, from the start of each run on the same workstation, one run at a time and each capped at 10 minutes, the hierarchy on the translations against the rigid-body coarse level for the displacement field:

  • the L-shaped concrete panel (examples/WinklerL, quadratic elements, damage): with the rigid-body coarse level the run completed in 581 s, while the translations had covered 88% of the load path when stopped at 600 s; 24.7 against 28.3 outer iterations per solve, at most 61 against one solve at the cap of 100. The load-displacement curves agree to 2e-4 of the peak load.

  • a coarser tunnel segment with embedded bars with bond-slip (0.28 M unknowns, SchurComplement, the rigid-body coarse level on the displacement field of the remaining nodes), run to its end: 46 against 65 s of computation, 13 against 21 Newton iterations and linear solves, and 9.1 against 14.7 outer iterations per solve. The final load agrees to 2e-4.

  • the damage cylinder (0.84 M unknowns, implicit dynamics): simulated time 0.215 against 0.185 in the same time, and 7.9 against 12.6 outer iterations per solve. The load agrees to 3e-4 at the same simulated times.

The corner-node two-level cycle with scaled weights and a composed coarse solve

See p-multigrid: TwoLevel(coarseSpace=CornerNodeInterpolation(coarseSolve)).

Offline, outer iterations and solve time (setup included) to a relative residual of 1e-6 on the displacement block alone, one cycle per outer iteration, on the captured systems of The rigid-body coarse level: the tunnel segment, elastic and damaged, and sequences of the damage cylinder and of the edge breakout.

Against the former corner-node two-level cycle – the plain weights \(\tfrac12, \tfrac12\) and an AMG coarse solve inside its C++ cycle – the scaled weights with the coarse solve DiagonalScaling(TwoLevel(coarseSpace=RigidBodyModesPerAggregate())) needed many times fewer outer iterations on the tunnel segment, several times fewer on the damage cylinder and fewer on the edge breakout, and less time on every system. They also needed fewer outer iterations than the rigid-body coarse level on the whole field on every system; with the fine spectral radius estimated by fewer power iterations than the default trees of the time, they were also faster on almost every system, while those long power iterations, on the fine level of the cycle above all, took back much of that gain in setup (the estimate that replaced them: The spectral radius estimate of the Chebyshev smoother). The iterations agree with those of the offline prototype the method was developed in. Calling the coarse component back from the C++ cycle, with the restriction and the prolongation, is a small part of an application next to the fine Chebyshev sweep.

Other coarse solves on the corner-node field: TwoLevel(coarseSpace=RigidBodyModesPerAggregate()) without the second scaling needed the same iterations where the scaling varies little along the edges (the tunnel, the cylinder), and many more on the edge breakout, where midside diagonals are far smaller than their corners’; DiagonalScaling(AMG(backendBlockSize=1)), an AMG hierarchy with the rigid-body modes of the corners, needed as many or more iterations and more time on every system.

These measurements were taken with the smoother of the time, degree 16 on both levels of the corner-node two-level cycle; the default is now degree 8 (The smoother of the corner-node two-level cycle).

Rebuilding the preconditioner for every solve

See Per-field AMG hierarchies: AMG and the reuse rules on Theory: the field-split AMG preconditioner.

Live, on the contact anchor model with many multi-point-constraint slaves, over a window of increments: two runs from the same checkpoint, the reuse rules of RebuildPolicy against reusePreconditioner=False, a new preconditioner for every solve. The two runs took slightly different paths (other hierarchies, other linear solutions within the tolerance), with about the same Newton iterations and simulated time reached.

A new preconditioner for every solve was faster over the window. A reused preconditioner needed several times the outer iterations of a fresh one, and a share of its solves stopped at the iteration cap, against none with a new preconditioner for every solve; the far cheaper Krylov solves outweighed the more frequent builds. Offline replays of captured solves had shown the opposite, a reused preconditioner only a few iterations behind a fresh one and reuse faster: they did not reproduce how far a live Jacobian moves between solves. Not yet measured live on a second model.

The smoother of the corner-node two-level cycle

See p-multigrid: TwoLevel(coarseSpace=CornerNodeInterpolation(coarseSolve)) and Default field trees.

The Chebyshev smoother – its degree, the lower end of its interval relative to the spectral radius, and the cycle, V(0,1) or V(1,1) – was swept for the three displacement preconditioners, AMG(), TwoLevel(coarseSpace=RigidBodyModesPerAggregate()) and TwoLevel(coarseSpace=CornerNodeInterpolation()), offline on the displacement block alone: LGMRES(DiagonalScaling(.)) to a true relative residual of 1e-6, setup included, first on three systems (the damaged tunnel lining, a late solve of the damage cylinder and of the edge breakout), then on the systems of The corner-node two-level cycle with scaled weights and a composed coarse solve and two post-onset cylinder solves. Every solve reached the tolerance. The smoother of CornerNodeInterpolation was set on both of its levels (the fine level and the rigid-body coarse level of its coarse solve), the others on every level they smooth.

  • For AMG() and the rigid-body coarse level, degree 16 is within the run-to-run scatter of the best setting. The optimum is flat along a ridge: a wider interval needs a higher degree (degree 16 on 0.01 to 1.1, degree 8 on 0.03 to 1.1 and V(1,1) with degree 4 on 0.03 to 1.1 tie). A lower degree made the tunnel lining faster and the late cylinder solves slower, at more than twice the iterations, and moved no time overall. They keep degree 16.

  • For the corner-node two-level cycle, degree 16 is stronger than it pays. The optimum is flat over degrees 6 to 10 and lower ends 0.02 to 0.05; degree 8 on 0.03 to 1.1 was faster on both levels than on the fine level alone.

  • A lower end of 0.1 lost everywhere; V(0,1) beat V(1,1) at the same number of polynomial applications for all three preconditioners.

  • The outer iterations of the corner-node two-level cycle did not depend on the number of threads, and the gain was larger at fewer threads.

On all systems, the default TwoLevel(coarseSpace=CornerNodeInterpolation()) (degree 8 on 0.03 to 1.1) against the former default written out, TwoLevel(coarseSpace=CornerNodeInterpolation(DiagonalScaling(TwoLevel(coarseSpace=RigidBodyModesPerAggregate(), amgclTree=t))), amgclTree=t) with t = defaultFieldTree(3), needed more outer iterations on every system, but fewer than twice as many, at half the matrix products per smoothing sweep: it was faster or as fast on every system, most on the tunnel lining and the damage cylinder; the edge breakout, whose time is mostly the setup, ties. The iterations of AMG() and of TwoLevel(coarseSpace=RigidBodyModesPerAggregate()) with their defaults are unchanged by this default.

Not yet measured live: with Newton in the loop, and with the hierarchies reused over several solves.

The two-level cycle written out

See The two-level cycle: two ways, on purpose.

The corner-node two-level cycle in C++, TwoLevel(coarseSpace=CornerNodeInterpolation(coarseSolve)), against the same cycle written out, Multiplicative([CoarseCorrection(cornerNodeInterpolation, coarseSolve), smoother]): the same \(P\) (bit for bit), the same coarse solve, and as the smoother AMGCL’s Chebyshev relaxation (degree 8, on the scalar double-precision matrix: EdelweissFE has no Chebyshev component). Offline, on the displacement blocks of three captured systems (a tunnel lining, a damage cylinder, an edge breakout), each inside LGMRES(DiagonalScaling(.)) to \(10^{-6}\), in repetitions.

Both forms need the same iterations on every system, and the written-out cycle takes markedly more wall time, in its applications and in its set-up. Forming CoarseCorrection’s products with \(P\) and \(P^T A P\) threaded, instead of in SciPy, made each of them several times faster and the written-out cycle faster as a whole, most in the set-up, but not as fast as the C++ cycle. In one application of the written-out cycle the smoother takes most of the time; the residual of Multiplicative and the coarse correction take a small share each. The C++ cycle smooths on its fine level stored in single-precision 3x3 node blocks, half the bytes per product; the same C++ cycle on double-precision scalar entries (backendBlockSize=1, backendPrecision="double") was slower per application on every system. What remains of the difference is that smoother, and in the set-up the bench script’s own search for the Dirichlet rows in NumPy, on one thread.

Why the defaults are what they are

The defaults of the blockAMG() preset are not a separate “recommended configuration” layered on top of different shipped defaults – they are the code’s defaults (those of blockAMG() and of its components). They are the winner of a systematic comparison, against the former defaults, on two captured models (a gradient-enhanced damage cylinder through its damage onset, and a contact anchor model with many multi-point-constraint slaves):

  • 3x3 blocks stored in float (set up in double, see backendPrecision) cut the cost per outer iteration.

  • One high-degree post-smoothing sweep (npre = 0, npost = 1, Chebyshev degree 16, instead of a symmetric V(1,1) cycle at degree 5) and smoothed aggregation’s own spectral-radius estimate for its prolongation smoother (estimate_spectral_radius, 20 power iterations) cut outer iterations for the same number of preconditioner applications.

  • The translations of the equilibrated block as a block hierarchy’s near-null-space (nearNullspace, the default on a block backend), instead of AMGCL’s identity blocks, which are \(D\)-weighted translations (see the near-null-space of a block backend). Measured later than the rest, live on more models than these two, with and without them: fewer outer iterations and fewer solves stopping at the iteration cap on most models, with the same physical results. The exceptions are the damage cylinder after its damage onset, with more cap hits at the same outer iterations per simulated time, and the contact model, with more outer iterations at fewer Newton iterations; both ran no slower. The translations are the near-null-space of the elastic operator, and help less on a softening, indefinite one.

  • The rigid-body coarse level for the 3D displacement field (rigidBodyModesPerAggregate), which holds the rotations the translations cannot: offline, on captured systems of three models and of the testfile deck, never more outer iterations than the hierarchy on the translations and up to three times fewer on a bending-dominated one, at a setup of up to 1.2 times; live, faster on all three models measured, with the same results (see Rigid-body coarse level: TwoLevel(coarseSpace=RigidBodyModesPerAggregate())).

  • LGMRES(restart=50) matches the outer-iteration cap (maxIterations = 100), so LGMRES restarts at most once within it; augmentationVectors only allocates its augmentation vectors without ever exhausting them before the cap.

  • RebuildPolicy(stalenessFactor=3), where hierarchies are reused (reusePreconditioner=True; since Rebuilding the preconditioner for every solve not the default), reuses them longer than the former factor of 1.5, which fired on noise at small iteration counts; with the cap of 100 it can fire only while the solve the hierarchies were built for took fewer than about 34 iterations – beyond that, only a cap hit (or a new increment, a pattern change) rebuilds them.

  • EisenstatWalkerTolerance(maximum=1e-3) is not about the linear solver at all: through a damage onset, Newton needs linear solves about ten times tighter than the Eisenstat–Walker forcing allowed at a maximum of 1e-2. minimum = 1e-4 was measured against 1e-3: the looser value cost Newton extra iterations already before the damage onset, and more wall-clock to match.

  • krylovOnScaledSystem = false converges the true residual directly, so the gap compensation and the true-residual restarts of the scaled system have nothing to do and are left at their own, otherwise inert, defaults.

  • pruneCondensedMatrixZeros (a nonlinear-solver option, not a component’s) now defaults to “pruned only for a solver that needs it”: a direct factorization does, a tree of Krylov solvers and AMG does not (same iterations with and without the stored zeros), and one whose Krylov solve reuses its preconditioner never has it – so on a model with multi-point constraints the hierarchies can be reused (reusePreconditioner=True) without more settings; the former setup needed this set by hand (see the reuse rules on Theory: the field-split AMG preconditioner).

Measured live, from the same checkpoints, the defaults against the former defaults (no per-model settings in either run): the contact model’s increments finished markedly faster; on the damage cylinder, the former defaults had still not cleared the damage-onset increment in a comparable time budget, while the current defaults carried the run through it and beyond.

How the settings got there

How the settings got there, live against the production settings the reference runs had used: both the contact model and the damage cylinder solved faster, judged per Newton iteration on the cylinder, with a forcing maximum of 1e-2 and on the scaled system. On the cylinder that setting took Newton more iterations per increment before the damage onset and failed the onset increment outright (a cutback); a maximum of 1e-3 restores the fewer-iteration path, converges the onset increment and reaches the same load, and is neutral on the contact model. krylovOnScaledSystem = false then saves a further amount on the cylinder, with fewer Newton iterations, and on the contact model. Offline, on captured consecutive systems of both models, the same configuration with a 200-iteration cap needed less wall-clock than with the displacement blocks stored in double, at identical outer iteration counts.

All of the above, except the offline 200-iteration-cap comparison just above, was measured with the outer-iteration cap at 50 (maxIterations). The default cap is 100 instead: more forgiving on a model that has not yet been characterised on this solver, at the cost of a capped solve possibly spending up to twice the iterations before its hierarchies are rebuilt.
