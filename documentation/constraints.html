Constraints

Constraints are defined globally, and may be used for introducing additional scalar equations into the global equation system.

Example syntax:

*constraint, type=rigidbody, name=myRigigBody
    nSet=right
    referencePoint=rBottom

linearizedrigidbodympc - Linearized rigid body constraints by DOF elimination

Module edelweissfe.constraints.linearizedrigidbodympc

Linearized rigid body constraint enforced by master-slave DOF elimination (multi-point constraint): the kinematics of linearizedrigidbody, without its Lagrange multipliers, so without extra DOFs and without a saddle-point system (iterative linear solvers such as blockamg need a nonzero diagonal).

The displacement of every slave node \(s\) follows the displacement and the (small) rotation of the reference point (RP),

\[\mathbf{u}_s = \mathbf{u}_{RP} + \boldsymbol{\phi}_{RP} \times \mathbf{d}_s, \quad \mathbf{d}_s = \mathbf{x}_s - \mathbf{x}_{RP},\]

in 3D (rotation field with 3 components), and in 2D (one rotation \(\phi\) about z)

\[u_{s,x} = u_{RP,x} - \phi \, d_{s,y}, \quad u_{s,y} = u_{RP,y} + \phi \, d_{s,x}.\]

The slave displacements are eliminated; the displacement and the rotation of the RP are the masters. Prescribe Dirichlet conditions on the RP only, not on the slave nodes: a slave degree of freedom that is also prescribed loses its record to the Dirichlet condition (reported by the solver), so the body is no longer rigid at that node.

This constraint is a MeshDependent: if adaptive refinement changes the slave node set (e.g. by hadaptivity), the records are rebuilt from the current members, so new nodes are tied as well.

Limitation: static solvers only. The RP carries no mass of its own, and both dynamic solvers check the assembled inertia before the multi-point constraint enters, so they reject the RP’s degrees of freedom (NID: no mass on a time-integrated DOF, as for linearizedrigidbody; NED: an inertia on only some DOFs of a field). For NED, folding the slave masses onto the RP would not suffice: the row-sum lumping used for ties gives the RP rotation the signed static moment of the slave masses instead of a rotational inertia.

nodetodiscreterigidbodypenalty - Contact against a discrete rigid body (deprecated)

Deprecated since version 26.11: Use surfacetodiscreterigidbodypenalty below. On quadratic (hexa20/quad8) slave faces a node-based penalty cannot transmit the tensile corner loads of a uniform pressure, so the corner nodes lift off (see Measured: the discrete solution lifts the corners off instead), and its per-node penalty makes the contact stiffness depend on the mesh.

Module edelweissfe.constraints.nodetodiscreterigidbodypenalty

This is a frictionless, penalty-based, unilateral (contact-only) constraint that prevents the nodes of a slave node set from penetrating the moving surface of a discrete rigid body. It is the discrete-mesh counterpart of nodetorigidsurfacepenalty (which uses an analytical boundary) and reuses the rigid body’s own closest-point surface query for the geometry evaluation. Currently 3D only, and implicit / explicit-static only (see Solvers).

Theory

Let a slave node \(s\) have reference position \(\mathbf{X}_s\) and displacement DOF \(\mathbf{u}_s\), so its current position is \(\mathbf{x}_s = \mathbf{X}_s + \mathbf{u}_s\). The rigid body carries a reference point (RP) with displacement DOF \(\mathbf{u}_{RP}\) and a rotation pseudo-vector DOF \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{RP}\); its current position is \(\mathbf{x}_{RP} = \mathbf{X}_{RP} + \mathbf{u}_{RP}\) and its orientation is \(\mathbf{R} = \exp(\mathrm{skew}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{RP}))\) (see Rigid bodies for the rigid-body kinematics).

For each slave node the rigid body’s surface query returns the signed distance \(d_s\) (negative when the node is inside the body) and the outward unit normal \(\mathbf{n}_s\) of the closest surface point, evaluated for the body’s current pose. Contact is active whenever \(d_s < 0\), with gap (penetration)

\[g_s = -d_s \; > 0 .\]

The penalty normal force is, for the two available type formulations,

\[f_n = k\, g_s \quad(\texttt{linear},\ \text{constant tangent } k), \qquad f_n = \tfrac{1}{2}\, k\, g_s^2 \quad(\texttt{quadratic},\ \text{tangent } k\, g_s),\]

with \(k\) the user-supplied penalty.

With the contact-point moment arm \(\mathbf{r}_s = \mathbf{x}_s - \mathbf{x}_{RP}\) about the current RP, the (negative) gap gradient with respect to the involved DOFs \((\mathbf{u}_s,\, \mathbf{u}_{RP},\, \boldsymbol{\theta}_{RP})\) defines the generalized direction vectors

\[\begin{split}\mathbf{w}_s = -\mathbf{n}_s , \qquad \mathbf{w}_{RP} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{n}_s \\[2pt] \left(\mathbf{R}\, \mathbf{J}_r(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{RP})\right)^{\!\top} (\mathbf{r}_s \times \mathbf{n}_s) \end{bmatrix},\end{split}\]

and, since the contact force on the nodes is \(-f_n\, \mathbf{w}\), the residual contribution – its negative, see The residual and the tangent – and its tangent are assembled exactly as in the node-to-rigid-surface penalty constraint,

\[\mathbf{P} \mathrel{+{=}} f_n\, \mathbf{w} , \qquad \mathbf{K} \mathrel{+{=}} k\, (\mathbf{w} \otimes \mathbf{w}) + f_n\, \nabla \mathbf{w} ,\]

per slave node, where \(\mathbf{w}\) collects the slave and RP blocks.

The factor \(\mathbf{J}_r(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{RP})\) is the right Jacobian of the SO(3) exponential map,

\[\mathbf{J}_r(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbf{I} - \frac{1-\cos\varphi}{\varphi^2}\,\mathrm{skew}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) + \frac{\varphi-\sin\varphi}{\varphi^3}\,\mathrm{skew}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^2 , \qquad \varphi = \lVert \boldsymbol{\theta} \rVert .\]

It is required because the stored rotation DOF \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{RP}\) is the raw total pseudo-vector (fed through the exponential map every iteration), and the exponential map does not compose additively: incrementing \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{RP}\) by \(\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}\) does not produce a physical spin of \(\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}\) unless the body is currently unrotated. The map \(\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{J}_r\) converts a perturbation of the stored DOF into the physical spatial rotation it actually produces; it reduces to the identity at \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{RP} = \mathbf{0}\), where \(\mathbf{w}_{RP}\) collapses to the naive \(\mathbf{r}_s \times \mathbf{n}_s\). Omitting it makes the RP-moment term a few percent off at \(\sim 0.1\) rad accumulated rotation and \(\sim 15\%\) off at \(\sim 0.4\) rad.

Both \(\mathbf{n}_s\) and \(\mathbf{r}_s\) are recomputed from the current, total solution every Newton iteration (no per-increment caching), so the coupling between the RP DOFs and the contact residual is exact. Because the rigid surface is a triangulated (piecewise-planar) mesh, \(\mathbf{n}_s\) is exactly constant within a facet — the intra-facet curvature term of the tangent vanishes identically — but it still rotates rigidly with the RP. The geometric-stiffness term \(f_n\, \nabla \mathbf{w}\) accounts for that rigid rotation of the normal and for the \(\mathbf{J}_r\) dependence, giving the exact linearization of \(\mathbf{w}\) in the implemented blocks (verified against finite differences for arbitrary accumulated rotation). The \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{RP}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{RP}\) self-block, which would require the second derivative of the exponential map, is omitted; this affects only the convergence rate, not the correctness of the converged solution. The per-block breakdown of the tangent is given in the inline comments of the computeKernels method in the module source.

surfacetodeformablesurfacepenalty - Integrated surface-to-surface contact

The integrated (Gauss-point-to-segment) counterpart of the constraint above: contact is evaluated at quadrature points over the slave surface’s facets, and the resulting pressure is distributed with each side’s parent element face shape functions rather than with the flat facets’ own.

Use this instead of nodetodeformablesurfacepenalty on quadratic (hexa20/quad8) contact surfaces. There, a node-based penalty scheme cannot transmit a correct pressure at all – the consistent nodal load of a uniform pressure at a serendipity corner is tensile, which a unilateral spring cannot exert, so the discrete solution opens every corner gap instead. The mechanism, the measurements, and why no choice of nodal weights can repair it are documented in Measured: the discrete solution lifts the corners off instead; the integrated formulation and its own limits are in Integrated contact: distributing with the parent face’s basis. On matched linear faces the parent-face basis coincides with the facet basis, so the two constraints agree to 13 digits and there is nothing to gain – but on non-matching meshes the integrated formulation is a substantial improvement at any element order, linear included, as the figure in Integrated contact: distributing with the parent face’s basis shows.

Scope: normal penalty contact under sliding=small, on both the implicit and the explicit solver path. Coulomb friction and augmented Lagrange are not implemented here (the node-based constraint has both), and sliding=finite is rejected rather than approximated.

tie - Surface-to-surface tie (DOF elimination)

An Abaqus-style tie constraint bonding a slave surface rigidly to a deformable master surface. Unlike the penalty- and Lagrange-multiplier-based constraints above, the tie is enforced by master-slave DOF elimination (multi-point constraint condensation): each slave node’s displacement is constrained to the frozen facet-linear interpolation of its closest master facet, \(u_s = \sum_a N_a \, u_{m_a}\), and the slave DOFs are condensed out of the equation system – exact enforcement, no penalty parameter, no additional DOFs, zero added stiffness. The same mechanism serves the implicit solvers (system matrix transformation \(\tilde{K} = T^T K \, T + I_s\), the identity \(I_s\) filling the structurally zero slave rows and columns, followed by the recovery \(\delta u_s \mathrel{+}= \sum_a N_a \, \delta u_{m_a}\) after each linear solve – symmetric whenever \(K\) is, and free of any scale of its own, so it equilibrates cleanly also for a consistent mass matrix; see MultiPointConstraintTransformation) and explicit dynamics (mass-conserving row-sum folding of slave masses and forces onto the masters, direct kinematic slaving – the critical time step is untouched).

The implicit solver’s useThreadedMPCCondensation option (Solvers, NIST) computes \(T^T K T + I_s\) in place, with an OpenMP-threaded kernel (MPCCondensation), instead of with SciPy’s single-threaded CSR routines, which build a second matrix: the system matrix is held once, not twice. For this, the CSR pattern of \(K\) gains, once per equation system, the entries where a master collects the couplings of its slaves (condensedPatternFillIn()); the slave columns of \(K\) become stored zeros in turn. Only the rows a constraint touches are computed, each by one thread from the rows of \(K\) it combines, in a fixed order of summation, so the result does not depend on the number of threads. Explicit zeros are pruned from a copy according to pruneCondensedMatrixZeros. It is off by default.

On matching interface meshes, tying is exactly equivalent to merging the interface nodes: the patch test passes to machine precision, and both the implicit and the explicit (central difference) solutions reproduce the monolithic mesh identically. On non-matching meshes the constraint is still enforced exactly, but the classical node-to-surface limitation applies: the slave surface’s consistent nodal force pattern, redistributed by the facet-linear weights, does not match the master’s own consistent pattern, so a uniform stress state exhibits a bounded, refinement-convergent interface perturbation (quantified in testfiles/edelweiss-only/TieHexa20Patch/test_mismatched.inp) – the same obstruction that motivates mortar methods.

For quadratic (hexa20/quad8) faces, generate both facet surfaces with triangulation=midside: the corner triangulation excludes the midside nodes from the facet node lists entirely, leaving slave midside nodes untied.

Contested slave degrees of freedom

A degree of freedom can be condensed out only once, so when several multi-point constraints ask for the same slave DOF, exactly one may keep it. This arises routinely under \(h\)-adaptivity: a hanging node created on a refined tie interface is a hangingnode slave, and the tie would like to constrain it too.

The decision is made centrally, where every constraint’s records are collected, and not inside any constraint: the first constraint in model order keeps the DOF, and later claims on it are dropped and reported. Resolving it centrally is what makes the result independent of the order in which constraints are refreshed – a constraint that inspected its peers mid-refresh would read some of them in their pre-refinement state.

Model order therefore carries meaning. For the hanging-node case the precedence is not arbitrary, and it is expressed by hAdaptivity registering its hanging-node constraint at the front of the model’s multi-point constraints:

  • If the hanging-node constraint keeps the node, the refined and unrefined meshes stay exactly conforming, and the node is still tied – indirectly but almost exactly, because its coarse parent nodes are themselves tie slaves and it is their interpolation.

  • If the tie keeps it instead, the node is bonded exactly, but nothing holds it on its coarse parent edge any more, and the refined and unrefined meshes come apart there.

One constraint has a stand-in for its condition and the other does not, which is what settles it. Measured on the coarse anchor pry-out, the two precedences differ by 5.3e-02 relative displacement and eventually by the refined mesh itself.

Note

This used to be resolved inside the tie, which built its records against its peers’ claimedSlaveNodes() while the refresh sweep was still running – so peers that had not yet refreshed answered with their pre-refinement claims. A node that had been a hanging node before the latest refinement, and was not one after it, was reported as still claimed, and the tie dropped it, leaving it constrained by nothing at all.

Multi-point constraints and Dirichlet boundary conditions

A degree of freedom eliminated by a multi-point constraint – a tie slave, a hangingnode slave – cannot also be prescribed by a Dirichlet boundary condition: the constraint already determines it from its masters, and a prescribed value would be a second, independent equation for the same unknown. Where a boundary condition’s node set overlaps a constraint’s slave nodes, the solvers resolve the overlap automatically: the conflicting constraint equation is dropped, for that component only, so that the boundary condition takes precedence. This is how Abaqus resolves the same overlap, and it needs no configuration.

The alternative – rejecting such a model and requiring the user to subtract the constraint’s slave nodes from the boundary condition’s node set – is worth understanding, because input files written for solvers that demand it often carry such a subtraction. It is a snapshot: computed against one mesh, one tie tolerance and one refinement state, and silently no longer correct when any of them changes, with no diagnostic, because what remains is a plain list of node labels that no longer records why those labels were chosen. It also interferes with \(h\)-adaptivity, which extends a node set to newly created nodes only where the whole parent face or edge already lies within the set: every face touching a subtracted node stops propagating the boundary condition to its children, so the boundary condition thins out with each refinement. Such subtractions should be removed rather than maintained.

Dropped equations are reported in two classes, and the distinction matters:

redundant

Every master carrying non-negligible weight is itself prescribed, and the weighted sum reproduces the value prescribed on the slave. The constraint equation and the boundary condition say the same thing, so dropping either changes nothing. Reported as a count, at journal level 1. This is the expected case for a symmetry plane cutting through a tied interface: the slave and its masters all lie on the plane and all carry the same prescribed value.

not implied

They do not say the same thing – some master is unprescribed, or the weighted sum disagrees. The boundary condition still wins, but the model has genuinely changed, so this is reported as a warning naming how much of the constraint weight rests on unprescribed masters. It is a signal worth chasing rather than silencing: the usual cause is a boundary condition whose node set is incomplete, so that masters which ought to carry the condition do not.

Masters are resolved transitively before classification – a master may itself be a slave of another constraint – and weights are compared against a scaled tolerance, never against exact zero, since clamped closest-point projections routinely leave weights at round-off magnitude.

Implementing your own constraints

Subclass from the constraint base class in module edelweissfe.constraints.base.constraintbase and implement its computeKernels. A constraint enters the equation system like an element does (see The residual and the tangent): it adds its residual contribution \(\mathbf{P}\) – the negative of the force it exerts on its nodes – and the tangent \(\mathbf{K} = \partial \mathbf{P} / \partial \mathbf{U}\). Two examples:

  • A penalty spring of stiffness \(k\) that pulls a degree of freedom \(u\) towards \(\bar{u}\) exerts the force \(-k\,(u - \bar{u})\) on its node, so it adds \(P \mathrel{+}= k\,(u - \bar{u})\) and \(K \mathrel{+}= k\), exactly as a spring element would. directionalspringpenalty is this constraint.

  • A Lagrange multiplier \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) enforcing \(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{0}\) adds \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^T \partial \mathbf{g} / \partial \mathbf{u}\) to the rows of the nodes and \(\mathbf{g}\) to the rows of the multipliers, with the tangent \(\begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\lambda}^T \partial^2 \mathbf{g} / \partial \mathbf{u}^2 & (\partial \mathbf{g} / \partial \mathbf{u})^T \\ \partial \mathbf{g} / \partial \mathbf{u} & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix}\). equalvaluelagrangian and rigidbody are such constraints.

Because \(\mathbf{K}\) is the derivative of exactly what is added to \(\mathbf{P}\), a constraint can be checked by finite differences of its own computeKernels: a wrong tangent, or a wrong sign of \(\mathbf{P}\) alone, shows up as a mismatch. \(\mathbf{P}\) and \(\mathbf{K}\) negated together pass this check, so the test of a constraint also checks the direction of the forces it exerts on its nodes: a penalty pulls them back towards what it prescribes, a contact pushes the bodies apart, a Lagrange multiplier acts on them as \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^T \partial \mathbf{g} / \partial \mathbf{u}\) with the constraint’s own \(\mathbf{g}\). tests/test_residual_tangent_convention.py does both for the constraints shipped with EdelweissFE (the two integrated surface contact constraints in their own test modules); add a case there for a new one. The helpers it uses are part of the package, so that the tests of a package with constraints of its own use them as well:

Helpers for checking the rule every constraint follows: it adds a residual contribution P and the tangent K = dP/dU (see the solver documentation, “The residual and the tangent”).

Because K must be the derivative of what is added to P, comparing it with central differences of P checks both at once: a wrong tangent, or a P whose sign alone is wrong, shows up as a mismatch. A P and a K negated together pass, so a test checks the direction of the forces the constraint exerts as well, which only the mechanics of the constraint can say: that a penalty pulls its nodes back, that a contact pushes the bodies apart.

They are part of the package, so that the tests of a package that adds constraints of its own can check them the same way as the tests of EdelweissFE check its constraints.
