Theory: the critical time step of an explicit analysis

An explicit analysis (NED, Solvers) solves no system of equations: it divides the net force by a lumped mass and steps on. The price is that it is stable only below a time increment, the critical time step. This page explains where that limit comes from, why EdelweissFE estimates it instead of computing it, how the estimates of the parts of a model are combined, what penalty constraints add to them, what the estimates do not see, and when each estimate is taken anew. The code is criticaltimestep.

Where the limit comes from

Linearize the equation of motion about the current state, \(\boldsymbol{M}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}} + \boldsymbol{C}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}} + \boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{u} = \boldsymbol{0}\), with the lumped (diagonal) mass \(\boldsymbol{M}\), damping \(\boldsymbol{C}\) and the tangent stiffness \(\boldsymbol{K}\). In the eigenvectors of \(\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}\) it falls apart into single oscillators \(\ddot{q} + \omega^2 q = 0\). The central difference method applied to one of them,

\[q_{n+1} - 2 q_n + q_{n-1} = -\omega^2 \Delta t^2 q_n ,\]

has the amplification factors \(z\) with \(z^2 - (2 - \omega^2\Delta t^2) z + 1 = 0\). Their product is one; they stay on the unit circle – the oscillation neither grows nor decays – as long as \(|2 - \omega^2 \Delta t^2| \le 2\), i.e. \(\omega \Delta t \le 2\). Beyond, one of them is real and larger than one, and that mode grows without bound. The whole system is stable if its highest mode is:

\[\Delta t \le \Delta t_\mathrm{crit} = \frac{2}{\omega_\mathrm{max}}, \qquad \omega_\mathrm{max}^2 = \lambda_\mathrm{max}(\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}) .\]

The damping does not lower this limit in NED, which takes the damping force with the mean of the velocities before and after the update. A field with a damping but no inertia (a first-order nonlocal field) is integrated by forward Euler, \(\boldsymbol{C}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}} + \boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{u} = \boldsymbol{0}\), stable for \(\Delta t \le 2/\lambda_\mathrm{max}(\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}\boldsymbol{K})\).

Why bounds, not eigenvalues

\(\omega_\mathrm{max}\) of the assembled system is one eigenvalue of a matrix the explicit method otherwise never assembles. Computing it – by a few power or Lanczos iterations on the assembled tangent – costs more than many increments, and it changes as the state changes. What the method needs is not the eigenvalue but a time increment that is safe: a lower bound of \(\Delta t_\mathrm{crit}\), from an upper bound of \(\omega_\mathrm{max}\), at a cost small against an increment. Two such bounds are used:

  • The element eigenvalue inequality. The highest frequency of an assembled mesh is not larger than the highest frequency of its elements, each taken alone with its own lumped mass: \(\omega_\mathrm{max}^2 \le \max_e \lambda_\mathrm{max}(\boldsymbol{M}_e^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}_e)\) (the Rayleigh quotient of the mesh is a weighted mean of the elements’ quotients). An element’s eigenvalue problem is small, and Marmot bounds it with the element’s own strain operator and the largest wave speed of its material, which holds for every element order and distortion of the geometry it is taken on – where the classic “element length over wave speed” is exact only for a linear bar. A finite-strain element of Marmot takes its reference geometry, with the wave speed at its current deformation: for a strongly deformed element, that no longer bounds its eigenvalue, and the elements’ policy rests on an assumption (see “When an estimate is taken anew”).

  • Row sums. The particles of a meshfree model (RKPM) have no such inequality: the stiffness of a particle couples nodes at which its own lumped mass vanishes. Every eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of a matrix is bounded by its largest absolute row sum (Gershgorin), and more sharply by the Collatz-Wielandt bound of its absolute value with positive weights. EdelweissMeshfree applies it to the mass-scaled stiffness \(\boldsymbol{M}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{M}^{-1/2}\) with weights per field – or, with carry-critical-time-step-weights, with weights carried from estimate to estimate and refined by one step of the power iteration each time (CriticalTimeStepOfEntitiesWithOwnInertia, “Carried weights”) –, and Marmot bounds the row sums of every particle without assembling its stiffness (EdelweissMeshfree, MPMDiscreteSystem.criticalTimeStepOfEntitiesWithOwnInertia).

Both are bounds from above for \(\omega_\mathrm{max}\), so the time increment they give is safe; how far below the true limit it lies is the price of not computing the eigenvalue.

Words. A bound is on the side of the eigenvalue: an upper bound of \(\omega_\mathrm{max}^2\), as the element eigenvalue inequality, Gershgorin’s or Collatz-Wielandt’s give it. An estimate is on the side of time: a part’s lower bound of its critical time step, made from such a bound (CriticalTimeStepEstimate). The time increment is what a step takes, at most courant-number times the critical time step. The code, its options and its journal use these words in this sense only.

How the parts combine

A model may hold finite elements and particles. Their degrees of freedom are disjoint – NED refuses a node that both would carry – so the matrix \(\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}\) of the model is block diagonal in the two parts, its eigenvalues are those of the parts, and the critical time step of the model is the smallest of the parts’ (as far as the parts’ own stiffness goes; what penalty constraints add is explained below):

\[\Delta t = \text{courant-number} \times \min\left(\Delta t_\mathrm{crit}^\mathrm{elements},\, \Delta t_\mathrm{crit}^\mathrm{particles}\right) .\]

courant-number is the one safety factor: the fraction of the estimated limit the analysis runs at. A part gives its estimate as an object (CriticalTimeStepEstimate), whose value is the part’s critical time step, and which, in a derived class, also holds what it was made from: what is added to a part’s stiffness can be added to its estimate there (CriticalTimeStepEstimateOfTheDofs). With penalty constraints, a third value joins the minimum (below). It is not the constraints’ own: it is the critical time step of the parts with the constraints’ rows added at the degrees of freedom they couple, never above the parts’ own estimates (CriticalTimeStepWithThePenaltyConstraints says why the minimum with the parts is kept all the same).

The particles’ estimate has one factor of its own, below one, which is not a safety factor but part of what it estimates: their stabilization (NSNI) acts on the increment, so their explicit scheme is not exactly the central difference method with their tangent, and it was measured to be stable up to a fraction of the tangent’s limit (EdelweissMeshfree, STABLE_FRACTION_OF_THE_TANGENT_LIMIT).

Penalty constraints

A penalty constraint – a contact, a support held by a penalty, a spring between two nodes – is a set of springs. A spring \(p\) measures a stretch \(g_p\), e.g. the gap of a contact point; the vector with which it acts on the degrees of freedom is the gradient of the stretch, \(\boldsymbol{a}_p = \partial g_p / \partial \boldsymbol{u}\), and a spring of stiffness \(k_p\) adds \(k_p\,\boldsymbol{a}_p\boldsymbol{a}_p^T\) to the stiffness of the nodes it couples. For a contact point, \(k_p\) is what the contact law gives the point at its gap: the penalty for the linear law, zero while the gap is open.

Such a constraint owns no degrees of freedom: it couples those of the parts, and has no critical time step of its own to take the minimum with – the spring adds to the frequencies of the nodes it couples. A mass \(m\) on a spring \(k_1\) (an element) and held by a penalty \(k_2\) oscillates with \(\omega^2 = (k_1 + k_2)/m\), not with the larger of the two. Adding the constraint’s largest frequency to the parts’ largest one would be safe but blunt: a stiff contact far from the smallest element would lower the time increment of the whole model.

The estimate: the constraint adds where it acts. For every degree of freedom \(i\) of the finite elements, let \(\omega_i^2\) be the largest \(\lambda_e = (2/\Delta t_e)^2\) of the elements at \(i\), and let

\[r_i = \frac{1}{\sqrt{M_i}} \sum_j |K^\mathrm{c}_{ij}| \frac{1}{\sqrt{M_j}}\]

be the row sum of the absolute value of the constraints’ stiffness, scaled by the lumped mass on both sides (every constraint gives it: addBoundOfAbsoluteStiffnessTimes). Then

\[\omega_\mathrm{max}^2 \le \Lambda = \max_i \left(\omega_i^2 + r_i\right) .\]

At a degree of freedom no constraint couples, \(r_i = 0\) and the elements’ estimate stands; a constraint lowers the critical time step only if the frequency it adds at its nodes, on top of the elements’ there, exceeds the largest one of the model. For a mass \(m\) held by a penalty \(k\) alone, \(r = k/m\) and the estimate is the exact \(2\sqrt{m/k}\); for two equal masses joined by a penalty, \(r = 2k/m\), again exact.

Why it is safe: lend the constraint a part of the mass. The argument of the element eigenvalue inequality holds for any split of the lumped mass among the pieces of the stiffness: if \(\boldsymbol{K} = \sum_a \boldsymbol{K}_a\) and \(\boldsymbol{M} = \sum_a \boldsymbol{M}_a\) (all diagonal, nonnegative), then

\[\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x} = \sum_a \boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{K}_a\boldsymbol{x} \le \sum_a \lambda_a\, \boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{M}_a\boldsymbol{x} \le \max_a \lambda_a \; \boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x} , \qquad \lambda_a = \lambda_\mathrm{max}(\boldsymbol{M}_a^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}_a) .\]

The constraint has no mass of its own, so it is lent the fraction \(\theta_i = r_i/\Lambda\) of the mass of every degree of freedom it couples, and the elements keep the rest, \((1 - \theta_i) M_i\).

  • The constraint with the masses \(\theta_i M_i\): by the row sums with the weights \(\sqrt{\theta_i}\) (Collatz-Wielandt), its largest eigenvalue is at most \(\max_i r_i/\theta_i = \Lambda\).

  • An element with the masses \((1-\theta_i)\) times its own: its eigenvalue grows at most by \(1/(1 - \max_{i \in e}\theta_i)\), and \(\lambda_e \le \omega_i^2 \le \Lambda - r_i = \Lambda (1 - \theta_i)\) at each of its nodes, so it stays below \(\Lambda\).

Every piece is below \(\Lambda\) with its share of the mass, so the whole is. The argument is only as safe as each \(\lambda_e\) is a bound of its element: the estimate is each element’s own, and the one of the Python elements lies above their exact critical time step (EdelweissFE#323). The argument is the Rayleigh quotient’s, which sees the symmetric part of a stiffness only, \(\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{x}^T\tfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{K} + \boldsymbol{K}^T)\boldsymbol{x}\); so \(r_i\) has to bound the row sums of the absolute value of the constraint’s symmetric part. A penalty spring is symmetric (a closed point with a frozen projection), and the elements’ estimate bounds their elastic stiffness; the tangent of a frictional contact while it slips is not (the coupling of its normal and tangential parts, Consistent tangent), and its symmetric part is bounded entry by entry by \(\tfrac{1}{2}(|\boldsymbol{K}^\mathrm{c}| + |\boldsymbol{K}^\mathrm{c}|^T)\), whose row sums the bound takes there (below, “From the tangent, or from the springs”).

The particles: the rows inside their field matrix. The tangent of gradient-enhanced particles is not symmetric, and their estimate is not a bound per degree of freedom: it is the Perron root of their field matrix \(\boldsymbol{Q}\) of the largest weighted row sums per block of fields (EdelweissMeshfree, CriticalTimeStepEstimateFromTheFieldMatrix), which bounds every eigenvalue of any matrix whose absolute value it bounds block by block. So the constraint’s rows go there. Entry by entry, \(|\boldsymbol{S} + \boldsymbol{R}| \le |\boldsymbol{S}| + |\boldsymbol{R}|\), with \(\boldsymbol{S}\) and \(\boldsymbol{R}\) the mass-scaled stiffness of the particles and of the constraint. A constraint that couples degrees of freedom of one field block \(g\) adds at most \(r_i\) to the weighted row sum of \(i\) within \(g\), where the weights are constant on that field; so \(Q_{gg}\) raised to \(\max_{i \in g} (Z_{ig} + r_i)\), with \(Z_{ig}\) the particles’ own row sum of \(i\) within \(g\), bounds the block row sums of \(|\boldsymbol{S}| + |\boldsymbol{R}|\), and the Perron root of a nonnegative matrix grows with its entries. This holds for any matrix, symmetric or not. With carried weights, the particles keep their weights constant per field in every field a constraint acts on, so that the rows add as they are.

A constraint that couples particles and finite elements at once – a projectile of elements on a slab of particles – is taken with the larger of the two: \(\Lambda\) of the elements, and the particles’ field matrix with the rows added. This is not proven: the elements’ argument needs the Rayleigh quotient, which the unsymmetric particles do not have, and the particles’ needs a bound per block, which the elements do not give.

With multi-point constraints (a tie of a fine mesh to a coarse one, hanging nodes), a slave moves with its masters, \(u_s = \sum_a N_{sa} u_a\), and the system that oscillates is the condensed one, \(\boldsymbol{T}^T \boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{T}\) over the independent degrees of freedom \(\boldsymbol{y}\), \(\boldsymbol{u} = \boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{y}\), with the slave masses folded onto the masters, \(\tilde{M}_m = M_m + \sum_s N_{sm} M_s\). A spring at a slave acts on its masters: its row sums are taken with the weights of the masters, \(w_s = \sum_a |N_{sa}| w_a\), and folded onto them by \(|\boldsymbol{T}|\), since \(|\boldsymbol{T}^T \boldsymbol{K}^\mathrm{c} \boldsymbol{T}| \le |\boldsymbol{T}|^T |\boldsymbol{K}^\mathrm{c}| |\boldsymbol{T}|\) entry by entry, whatever the signs of the coefficients (foldRowSumsOfAbsoluteCoefficients()).

So does an element at a slave: it acts on the masters through \(\boldsymbol{T}^T \boldsymbol{K}_e \boldsymbol{T}\), so a master takes the largest \(\lambda_e\) of the elements at it and at its slaves (foldLargestOntoMasters()), and the rows of the constraints meet it there. Without that fold, a stiff element at a slave would be missing at its master just where a penalty there adds the most. The element at the slave sees \(u_s^2\), which the masters must pay for with mass. With coefficients that are nonnegative and sum to one – a tie, the hanging nodes of linear elements – Jensen’s inequality, \((\sum_a N_{sa} u_a)^2 \le \sum_a N_{sa} u_a^2\), asks master \(m\) for \(N_{sm} M_s\), just what the fold of the mass gives it, and the argument above holds for the condensed system. The hanging nodes of a HEX20 or QUAD8 mesh are tied with the serendipity functions of the coarse face, which are negative at some masters (\(-1/8\) for a node at a quarter of a coarse edge, \(-1/4\) at the corners for one at the centre of a coarse face); Jensen’s inequality fails there, and that the elements’ estimates hold at their masters is not shown (below).

What the bound gives away is the Gershgorin step: the row sum is the exact frequency for springs between equal masses and above it otherwise, and a direction oblique to the axes spreads the spring over the components of a node.

From the tangent, or from the springs. By default, a constraint is bounded from the tangent it gives at the state (addBoundOfAbsoluteStiffnessTimes()): in every row, the larger of the row sums of \(|\boldsymbol{K}^\mathrm{c}|\), which the particles’ argument needs, and of \(\tfrac{1}{2}(|\boldsymbol{K}^\mathrm{c}| + |\boldsymbol{K}^\mathrm{c}|^T)\), which the elements’ argument needs for an unsymmetric tangent; for a symmetric tangent, the two are equal. A constraint whose tangent is costly, or that has none – one evaluated without a tangent in an explicit analysis –, gives its stiffness as springs instead: a spring \(p\) with the vector \(\boldsymbol{a}_p\) with which it acts and the stiffness \(k_p\) of its law – the normal spring of a closed point, a support’s spring along the axis it holds. The row sums are then, with the weights \(\boldsymbol{w}\) of the columns, \(b_i \mathrel{+}= \sum_p |k_p|\, |a_{p,i}|\, (|\boldsymbol{a}_p| \cdot \boldsymbol{w})\) (edelweissfe.constraints.base.penaltysprings), one formula for every such constraint, and the constraint takes the springs for its bound from the same code it takes them for its force, so that a change of the force cannot leave the bound behind.

At which state. The central difference method integrates in each increment the net force evaluated at the end of the increment before, \(\boldsymbol{v}_{n+1/2} = \boldsymbol{v}_{n-1/2} + \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t_n + \Delta t_{n+1})\, \boldsymbol{M}^{-1} \boldsymbol{f}_n\). The stiffness that limits \(\Delta t_{n+1}\) is the one \(\boldsymbol{f}_n\) was evaluated with: which points of a contact were closed, and how deep, and which points a contact search had paired. So NED bounds the constraints right after every evaluation of the net force, with the same state: at the start of a step, and at the end of every increment. The bound limits \(\Delta t_{n+1}\), and \(\boldsymbol{f}_n\) is integrated over the average with \(\Delta t_n\), which was taken with \(\boldsymbol{f}_{n-1}\). Whatever stiffness is new in \(\boldsymbol{f}_n\) is therefore half an increment late: a point that closed in increment \(n\), a point a contact search paired at its start. The first increment that integrates its force does so over the average of its own time increment and the one taken without it (see “What the estimate does not see” for what that means, and why it is accepted).

After a refinement, the net force of the new mesh starts at zero: the first increment on it integrates no force, and the one after integrates the first force on the new mesh over the average with the increment of the first. That increment is set at the refinement; with the constraints as last bounded, on the old mesh with its larger masses, it would be half an increment late in the same way. So NED bounds the constraints also right after a refinement, at the state on the new mesh, as at the start of a step, and both increments are taken with the masses of the new mesh.

What the estimate does not see

The parts’ estimates cover the stiffness of the parts themselves, and the penalty constraints add theirs where they act (above), within a part and between the parts. Not seen are:

  • Stiffness that is new in a net force: a point that closed in the increment that evaluated the force, a point a contact search paired at its start. It enters the estimate right after the force, and so the time increment of the next increment, but that increment integrates the force over the average of its own time increment and the one before, which was taken without the new stiffness (“At which state”): for one increment, the average, half-way between the two, may lie above the critical time step of the force it integrates. What this means: in that one increment, the oscillation of the new stiffness is integrated with too large a time increment, and grows once, by a factor of the order of \(\Delta t_n / \Delta t_{n+1}\), the ratio of the time increments before and after the new stiffness. Lowered only, the time increment is never raised again within the step, so every later increment is within the limit of that stiffness, and the growth does not compound: this is not an instability. Why it is accepted: avoiding it would need the stiffness of a force before the force is evaluated, a bound over the states the next increment can reach. The energy balance of NED (the kinetic energy may not exceed the energy at the start of the step plus the external work) remains the check. A stiffness that lowers the time increment by more than the margin of the courant-number in one increment – a penalty law whose stiffness grows with the penetration – exceeds the limit in that increment. With follow-critical-time-step, the time increment is raised again as soon as a contact has opened, and the next point that closes meets the full raised increment: there, take a lower courant-number.

  • The skew part of an unsymmetric tangent (a frictional contact while it slips): it does no work in \(\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}\) and is outside the argument above. Where it makes an eigenvalue of \(\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}\) complex, the central difference method grows at every time increment, as the motion of the model itself does (flutter): that is a property of the model, which no time increment cures.

  • The hanging nodes of HEX20 and QUAD8 meshes. Their multi-point coefficients are negative at some masters, where the fold of the slave masses gives a master less than the elements at its slaves need; that the elements’ estimates still bound the condensed system there is assumed, not shown, with or without penalty constraints.

  • A multi-point constraint whose coefficients do not sum to one (a linearized rigid body): the argument above assumes coefficients that sum to one.

  • The geometric stiffness of contact, for a constraint that bounds its springs: that of a contact with finite sliding, or of a rigid body whose normals rotate with it (of the order of the normal force over the size of a facet or the lever arm, small against the penalty). A constraint bounded from its tangent has in its bound what its tangent holds.

  • A constraint that couples particles and finite elements at once: taken with the larger of the two arguments, which is not proven (see “The particles”).

  • A penalty on a field integrated by forward Euler (a field with a damping and no inertia): its rows are added to no estimate.

  • A constraint that bounds nothing: EdelweissMeshfree’s ParticlePenaltyFacetContactExplicit gives neither a tangent nor its springs; its stiffness stays outside the estimate.

  • The tangent of a rate-dependent material. The stiffness the particles are estimated with is that of a zero increment over a unit time; for a material with a viscosity, e.g. Duvaut-Lions, it is relaxed over that time, softer than the response within an explicit increment, and the estimate may be too large.

  • The particles’ stabilization. The factor of the particles’ NSNI above was measured, on blocks of particles, not derived; a particle arrangement far from those may need a smaller one.

How to check: halve courant-number and compare the results, and watch the energy balance of NED: kinetic energy that appears from nowhere is the sign of a time increment above the true limit.

When an estimate is taken anew

The true critical time step changes as the analysis runs: elements are refined, particles move and deform, materials stiffen and soften. Estimating in every increment would always be right, but an estimate of all particles costs a noticeable part of an increment. So each part comes with a policy, decided by what its estimate depends on.

The finite elements: once per mesh, and at the start of every step (CriticalTimeStepOfTheElements). Their estimate depends on the mesh, on the lumped mass, and on the wave speed of the material at the state of the estimate. An estimate of all elements costs a full element pass, so the policy assumes that within a step neither the geometry nor the wave speed of an element changes enough to use up the margin the courant number leaves. A softening material only raises the true limit; the assumption fails where elements are compressed far at finite strain, which shrinks their geometry, or where their material stiffens with the deformation: a hyperelastic solid in compression, a compacting material. There, take a lower courant-number, or shorter steps, since every step estimates anew. The elements are estimated anew after a refinement, and at the start of every step, since a material property may have changed with the step (changeMaterialProperty).

The particles: when the margin may be used up (CriticalTimeStepOfEntitiesWithOwnInertia). Their estimate depends on their state: on their lumped masses, which change as they move over the nodes of the kernel functions, and on their stiffness, which changes with the material and with the deformation of the particles. After an estimate \(v\), NED keeps in every increment the critical time step at worst, \(L\): a lower bound of the particles’ critical time step now, \(n\) increments later,

\[L = v \, \min\left(1, \sqrt{\alpha}, \beta\right) (1 - \delta)^n ,\]

and takes a new estimate when \(\text{courant-number} \times L\) falls below the time increment in use.

  • \(\alpha = \min_i M_i / M_i^0 \le 1\) is the smallest ratio of a lumped mass now to the same mass at the estimate (1 if none fell), \(\beta \le 1\) the same for the damping of a forward-Euler field. This factor is exact. The estimate scales the stiffness with \(\boldsymbol{M}^{-1/2}\) on both sides; if every mass is at least \(\alpha\) times what it was, every scaled row sum, and with them the bound of \(\omega_\mathrm{max}^2\), is at most \(1/\alpha\) times larger (the largest eigenvalue of a nonnegative matrix grows with its entries), so the estimate is at least \(\sqrt{\alpha}\) times what it was. The damping enters linearly instead: the forward Euler limit \(2/\lambda_\mathrm{max}(\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}\boldsymbol{K})\) is linear in \(\boldsymbol{C}\), while the central differences’ \(2/\omega_\mathrm{max}\) goes with the square root of \(\boldsymbol{M}\). The masses cost nothing to watch: NED assembles them in every increment anyway. A node that gets a mass for the first time (a particle reaches a kernel function’s node, whose mass grows from zero) is a new row whose scaling has no bound: then the particles are estimated anew at once.

  • \(\delta\), the option critical-time-step-decrease-per-increment, is the largest relative fall per increment the stiffness may cause. This factor is an assumption. No theorem bounds it: the tangent of a material can change abruptly from one increment to the next. What makes it reasonable is what an increment is: a fraction of the time a wave needs to cross a particle, so in one increment the configuration moves by a fraction of a particle, and the stiffness, which follows the configuration, changes by a fraction of itself. Its default was measured: in the impact of a steel projectile on a concrete slab of gradient-enhanced particles, the fall of the particles’ estimate not explained by their masses stayed below half the default in every single increment, and far below it over several increments. There, the fall came from particles at the rim of the crater that are stretched far: the nonlocal field of a gradient-enhanced particle is balanced with gradients in the reference configuration, which grow with the stretch, while its micro-inertia does not – a smooth change from increment to increment, which only an estimate sees, not the masses. A larger \(\delta\) is more cautious and estimates more often; 1 estimates in every increment.

What the policy does in practice follows from the margin. Where the particles set the time increment, the increment in use is courant-number times their last estimate, the margin is used up after one increment, and they are estimated in every increment – as they must be, since their estimate is what limits the analysis. Where another part sets it, the estimate of the particles is above the increment in use by some factor \(m\), and it is taken anew only every \(\ln m / -\ln(1-\delta)\) increments, unless their masses fall faster than expected.

Where penalty constraints couple the particles, the same critical time step at worst is kept for the particles’ rows with the constraints added: the particles’ stiffness may have grown by \(1/f^2\), \(f = \min(1, \sqrt{\alpha}, \beta)(1 - \delta)^n\), the constraints’ rows are those of now. The particles are estimated anew also when courant-number times that one falls below the time increment in use – the margin a contact at the particles’ nodes leaves is smaller than their own.

The penalty constraints: after every evaluation of the net force (CriticalTimeStepWithThePenaltyConstraints). Their stiffness follows the state of the contact – which points are closed, and how deep, for a law whose stiffness grows with the penetration –, and changes from increment to increment; their row sums cost about as much as their force. So they are formed right after the net force, at the state it was evaluated at (see “At which state”): at the start of a step, after every increment, and after a refinement, at the state on the new mesh. They are added to the parts’ last estimates; only this addition is renewed, and the parts keep their own policies.

The code

The critical time step of an explicit analysis, estimated part by part.

The central difference method is stable for \(\Delta t \le 2/\omega_\mathrm{max}\), the critical time step, with \(\omega_\mathrm{max}^2\) the largest eigenvalue of \(\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}\). The explicit dynamic solver NED never solves that eigenvalue problem. It asks the parts of the model for an estimate of their own critical time step – a lower bound of it, from an upper bound of \(\omega_\mathrm{max}^2\) –, takes the smallest, and runs at the fraction courant-number of it. The parts have disjoint degrees of freedom, so the smallest of their critical time steps is the model’s.

A part gives its estimate as a CriticalTimeStepEstimate. A penalty constraint – a contact, a support, a tie held by a penalty – owns no degrees of freedom: it adds stiffness to the ones of the parts it couples. It has no critical time step of its own to take the minimum with; it adds to the parts’ estimates at the degrees of freedom it couples: a part gives its estimate for them as a CriticalTimeStepEstimateOfTheDofs, to which the row sums of the constraints’ mass-scaled stiffness are added.

An estimate costs something, and how much its value can change while the analysis runs depends on the part, so every part (CriticalTimeStepOfAPart) comes with its own policy of when its estimate is taken anew:

  • CriticalTimeStepOfTheElements: the finite elements, estimated once per mesh;

  • CriticalTimeStepOfEntitiesWithOwnInertia: the entities that carry their own inertia (the particles of a meshfree model), estimated anew when the margin of the last estimate over the time increment in use may be used up;

  • CriticalTimeStepWithThePenaltyConstraints: the parts with the penalty constraints, estimated after every evaluation of the net force, from the stiffness the constraints had in it. This third value is not the constraints’ own critical time step (see the class).

The theory, and what the estimates do not see, are explained in Theory: the critical time step of an explicit analysis.

class edelweissfe.solvers.base.criticaltimestep.CriticalTimeStepOfEntitiesWithOwnInertia(decreasePerIncrement, carryWeights=False)[source]

The critical time step of the entities that carry their own inertia (the particles of a meshfree model), a property of their assembled system at their current configuration (criticalTimeStepOfEntitiesWithOwnInertia()).

Policy: when the margin may be used up. It depends on the state of the entities – their stiffness, their positions, their lumped inertia and damping – and changes from increment to increment, but an estimate costs a pass over all of them. After an estimate \(v\), the solver therefore keeps, in every increment, the critical time step at worst, \(L\): a lower bound of the entities’ critical time step now, \(n\) increments after the estimate,

\[L = v \, \min\left(1, \sqrt{\alpha}, \beta\right) (1 - \delta)^n ,\]

and estimates anew as soon as the increment in use is no longer a courant-number fraction of it.

  • \(\alpha = \min_i M_i / M_i^0 \le 1\) is the smallest ratio of a lumped inertia now to the same inertia at the estimate (1 if none fell), over the degrees of freedom that had one then and have one now, and \(\beta\) the same for the damping of the degrees of freedom integrated by forward Euler (a damping, no inertia). This part is exact: the estimate scales the stiffness with \(\boldsymbol{M}^{-1/2}\) on both sides, so with the stiffness held, a fall of the masses to \(\alpha\) times raises the bound of \(\omega_\mathrm{max}^2\) at most \(1/\alpha\) times and lowers \(2/\omega_\mathrm{max}\) at most to \(\sqrt{\alpha}\) times; the forward Euler limit \(2/\lambda_\mathrm{max}(\boldsymbol{C}^{-1} \boldsymbol{K})\) is linear in the damping, so it falls at most to \(\beta\) times. The masses are known in every increment: the solver assembles them anyway.

  • \(\delta\) (option critical-time-step-decrease-per-increment) is the largest relative fall of the estimate per increment that its stiffness may cause. This part is an assumption, not a theorem: the tangent of a material can change abruptly, and the stiffness of a particle changes with its deformation. It rests on what an increment is – a fraction of the time a wave needs to cross an entity, so the configuration moves by a fraction of an entity – and on measurements, and it is documented with them in Theory: the critical time step of an explicit analysis.

The estimate is also taken anew when a degree of freedom has an inertia, or a damping alone, that it did not have at the last estimate (a node that a particle reaches for the first time: a new row whose mass starts from zero), after a new numbering, and in the first evaluation of a step. A degree of freedom that loses its inertia leaves the system and can only raise its critical time step.

Where the entities set the time increment, the margin is used up after one increment, and they are estimated in every increment; where another part sets it, they are estimated only every so many increments, as many as their margin allows at the rate \(\delta\).

Carried weights (option carry-critical-time-step-weights). The estimate of the entities is made from a Collatz-Wielandt bound with weights constant per field (criticalTimeStepOfEntitiesWithOwnInertia()). With carried weights, every estimate takes the weights the last one left and leaves the next ones, one step of the power iteration towards the Perron vector, where the bound is exact. Any positive weights bound the largest eigenvalue from above, but weights refined at another configuration can bound it less tightly than the weights constant per field they started from when the stiffness changed faster than the weights followed, e.g. where entities reach nodes whose weights the power iteration has made small. So an estimate with carried weights below the estimate the weights started from starts them anew: the entities are estimated again from the weights constant per field, and the larger of the two estimates is taken. Every estimate is thus at least the one the weights started from: with the time increment lowered only, it never falls below the smallest estimate with weights constant per field of the step; followed (follow-critical-time-step), it can fall below the estimate without carried weights of the same configuration, down to the one the weights started from. The weights start anew – constant per field – also with every numbering (the nodes they belong to may have changed) and every step (a material property may have changed), and after a resume: they are not part of the checkpoint.

class edelweissfe.solvers.base.criticaltimestep.CriticalTimeStepOfTheElements[source]

The critical time step of the finite elements, the smallest of the elements’ own estimates (computeCriticalTimeStepForExplicitDynamics): the largest eigenfrequency of an assembled mesh is bounded by the largest of its elements, so the smallest critical time step of an element is an estimate of the mesh’s.

Policy: once per mesh, and at the start of every step. An element’s estimate depends on the mesh, on its lumped mass and on the wave speed of its material at the state of the estimate; a finite-strain element of Marmot takes its reference geometry with the wave speed at its current deformation. The policy assumes that within a step neither the geometry nor the wave speed changes enough to use up the margin that the courant number leaves. A softening material only raises the true limit; the assumption fails where elements are compressed far at finite strain, or where their material stiffens with the deformation (a hyperelastic solid in compression, a compacting material). There, take a lower courant-number, or shorter steps, since every step estimates anew. So the elements are estimated anew when the mesh changes (a refinement) and at the start of every step, since a material property may have changed with the step; not for a new numbering of the same mesh, and never within an increment loop that does not change the mesh.

class edelweissfe.solvers.base.criticaltimestep.CriticalTimeStepWithThePenaltyConstraints[source]

The critical time step of the parts with the penalty constraints – contacts, supports and ties held by a penalty: the estimate of each part (CriticalTimeStepEstimateOfTheDofs) with the row sums of the constraints added at the degrees of freedom they couple,

\[\omega_\mathrm{max}^2 \le \max_i \left(\omega_i^2 + r_i\right), \qquad r_i = \frac{1}{\sqrt{M_i}} \sum_j |K^\mathrm{c}_{ij}| \frac{1}{\sqrt{M_j}} ,\]

with \(\omega_i^2\) the part’s bound of the squared frequency at the degree of freedom \(i\) and \(\boldsymbol{K}^\mathrm{c}\) the stiffness of the constraints (addBoundOfAbsoluteStiffnessTimes()). A constraint has no critical time step of its own: it raises the frequencies of the degrees of freedom it couples, so it lowers the critical time step only where it acts, and only by what it adds there.

The value is not the constraints’ own. It is the critical time step of the parts with the constraints’ rows added at the degrees of freedom they couple, so it is never above the parts’ own estimates as they were when it was taken; and a part estimated anew is estimated before it (the elements of a new mesh, the entities right before it), so it is never above their current ones either. The model’s critical time step is nevertheless the minimum of all three (NED._criticalTimeStepOfTheModel), so that it needs no knowledge of which part has constraints: without constraints this value is infinite – before their first estimate in a step, and from the estimate that finds the discrete system without any (forget()), e.g. once the last particle has left a wall, so that the time increment it lowered can follow the parts again.

Policy: after every evaluation of the net force. Increment \(n+1\) integrates the net force \(\boldsymbol{f}_n\), evaluated at the end of increment \(n\), over the average of its own time increment and the one before, \(\tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t_n + \Delta t_{n+1})\). \(\Delta t_{n+1}\) is set right after \(\boldsymbol{f}_n\), so it has to be within the critical time step of the stiffness that force was evaluated with. The stiffness of a constraint follows the state of the contact – which of its points are closed, and how deep –, which changes from increment to increment, and bounding it costs about as much as evaluating its force. So it is estimated right after the net force is, at the same state and on the same connectivity: at the start of a step and after every increment; and after a refinement, at the state on the new mesh, before the first increment on it. \(\Delta t_n\) was set with \(\boldsymbol{f}_{n-1}\): stiffness that is new in \(\boldsymbol{f}_n\) – a point that closed in increment \(n\), a point a contact search paired at its start – is half an increment late, since the average still holds an increment taken without it (Theory: the critical time step of an explicit analysis, “What the estimate does not see”).
