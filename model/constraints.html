Constraints

Constraints are defined globally, and may be used for introducing additional scalar equations into the global discrete system.

Example syntax:

*constraint, type=rigidbody, name=myRigigBody
    nSet=right
    referencePoint=rBottom

embeddedregion - Embedded elements (perfect bond of reinforcement)

Ties every node of the embedded elements (e.g., truss or beam elements of reinforcement bars or structural members, meshed independently of the host) to the host continuum element it lies in, \(u_s = \sum_a N_a(\xi_s)\, u_{a}\), by master-slave DOF elimination, like Abaqus’ *EMBEDDED ELEMENT.

The nodal P of an embedded (slave) node is reported on the slave node itself, before condensation: a reaction summed over a node set must include the embedded nodes on that boundary.

Module edelweissfe.constraints.embeddedregion

Embedded region constraint: perfect bond of embedded elements (e.g., reinforcement bars) to host continuum elements, enforced by master-slave DOF elimination (multi-point constraint), like Abaqus’ *EMBEDDED ELEMENT.

Every node of the embedded elements is tied to the host element it lies in,

\[\boldsymbol{u}_s = \sum_a N_a(\boldsymbol{\xi}_s) \, \boldsymbol{u}_a ,\]

with the parametric coordinates \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_s\) of the node in the host element, found once in the reference configuration (inverse isoparametric mapping, inverseMapping()). Each dependency is a record of a MultiPointConstraintBase: a row of the transformation \(\mathbf{T}\) that condenses the system to \(\mathbf{T}^\mathsf{T} \mathbf{K} \mathbf{T}\) (MultiPointConstraintTransformation). The embedded elements are meshed independently of the host mesh; they keep their own stiffness, which is added to the host’s.

Embedded beams (elements with a rotation field, e.g., Marmot B2D2, B3D3): their displacements are tied as above; their rotations as chosen by rotations. The rotation of an Euler-Bernoulli beam is the slope of its axis, \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_\perp = \boldsymbol{t} \times \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}/\mathrm{d}s\). An embedded axis moves with the host, \(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}/\mathrm{d}s = (\nabla \boldsymbol{u}) \, \boldsymbol{t}\), hence \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_\perp = \boldsymbol{t} \times (\nabla \boldsymbol{u} \, \boldsymbol{t})\). A material line carries no twist, so the twist comes from the infinitesimal host rotation \(\boldsymbol{\omega} = \tfrac{1}{2} \nabla \times \boldsymbol{u}\):

\[\boldsymbol{\theta}_s = \boldsymbol{t} \times ( \nabla \boldsymbol{u} \, \boldsymbol{t} ) + ( \boldsymbol{t} \cdot \boldsymbol{\omega} ) \, \boldsymbol{t} \qquad \text{(2D: } \theta_s = \boldsymbol{t} \times ( \nabla \boldsymbol{u} \, \boldsymbol{t} ) \cdot \boldsymbol{e}_z \text{)} .\]

Which mode?

free

displacements only (default; as Abaqus’ embedded elements): the beam resists bending only by the tied displacements of its nodes, i.e., by the curvature of its nodal polygon – an embedded cantilever is softer than a clamped one. Right for rebar-like beams.

axis

the rotation of the host material line along the beam axis (above): clamps the beam, consistent with the Euler-Bernoulli kinematics; a host in uniform strain (also in shear) leaves a straight embedded beam straight. For members carrying moments into the host.

host

the host rotation \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) itself: differs from axis where the host is strained – a uniform strain rotates every inclined material line (\(\boldsymbol{t} \times (\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \boldsymbol{t}) \neq \mathbf{0}\)) while \(\boldsymbol{\omega} = \mathbf{0}\), so inclined beams bend with the material rotation only.

At a beam node on a face, an edge or a node shared by host elements, \(\nabla \boldsymbol{u}\) jumps between them: the rotation operator is the average of those of all hosts containing the node (independent of the host order), and at a kink of a beam (two elements with different axes) the average of the operators of both axes. The axis of a 3-node beam is its tangent at the node.

Limitations: the host is located in the reference configuration; the rotation ties are linear (infinitesimal rotations, for the geometrically linear beams: the co-rotational beams B2D2CR, B3D2CR may only be embedded with rotations=free); the volume of the embedded material is not subtracted from the host; embedded nodes outside of all hosts raise an error, or are left free with allowUnembedded=True. The ties work with every solver that supports multi-point constraints (NIST, NID, NED; not NEST or the parallel arc-length solver). With explicit dynamics, the masses of the slave nodes are folded onto the host nodes; Marmot beams have no rotary inertia, so the rotation records fold none. Under adaptive mesh refinement of the host, the ties are rebuilt on the refined mesh.

    embeddedElSet=rebars, hostElSet=concrete

linearizedrigidbodympc - Linearized rigid body constraints by DOF elimination

Module edelweissfe.constraints.linearizedrigidbodympc

Linearized rigid body constraint enforced by master-slave DOF elimination (multi-point constraint): the kinematics of linearizedrigidbody, without its Lagrange multipliers, so without extra DOFs and without a saddle-point system (iterative linear solvers such as blockamg need a nonzero diagonal).

The displacement of every slave node \(s\) follows the displacement and the (small) rotation of the reference point (RP),

\[\mathbf{u}_s = \mathbf{u}_{RP} + \boldsymbol{\phi}_{RP} \times \mathbf{d}_s, \quad \mathbf{d}_s = \mathbf{x}_s - \mathbf{x}_{RP},\]

in 3D (rotation field with 3 components), and in 2D (one rotation \(\phi\) about z)

\[u_{s,x} = u_{RP,x} - \phi \, d_{s,y}, \quad u_{s,y} = u_{RP,y} + \phi \, d_{s,x}.\]

The slave displacements are eliminated; the displacement and the rotation of the RP are the masters. Prescribe Dirichlet conditions on the RP only, not on the slave nodes: a slave degree of freedom that is also prescribed loses its record to the Dirichlet condition (reported by the solver), so the body is no longer rigid at that node.

This constraint is a MeshDependent: if adaptive refinement changes the slave node set (e.g. by hadaptivity), the records are rebuilt from the current members, so new nodes are tied as well.

Limitation: static solvers only. The RP carries no mass of its own, and both dynamic solvers check the assembled inertia before the multi-point constraint enters, so they reject the RP’s degrees of freedom (NID: no mass on a time-integrated DOF, as for linearizedrigidbody; NED: an inertia on only some DOFs of a field). For NED, folding the slave masses onto the RP would not suffice: the row-sum lumping used for ties gives the RP rotation the signed static moment of the slave masses instead of a rotational inertia.

nodetodeformablesurfacepenalty - Node-to-deformable-surface contact

The complete theory of this constraint – surface discretization and triangulation, tributary areas, finite- and small-sliding gap kinematics, penalty and augmented-Lagrange normal laws, Coulomb friction with consistent tangents, solver integration, and the verification methodology – is documented in Contact mechanics: theory.

In a plane model each slave node stands for its tributary length times the thickness of the sections of its elements (Plane models: every point stands for the thickness of its section). An axisymmetric model (*job, domain=axisymmetric) is refused: the constraint is not verified on rings; use surfacetodeformablesurfacepenalty with contactPoints=nodes there.

Module edelweissfe.constraints.nodetodeformablesurfacepenalty

A penalty based unilateral contact constraint between a deformable slave surface and a deformable master surface, both represented by flat contact facet elements ( contactsurfaceelement, typically created via surfaceelementgenerator). The slave surface’s nodes act as the contact points; each slave node’s penalty force is weighted by its tributary area (the sum of measure / nFacetNodes over its incident slave facets, evaluated in the reference configuration, times the thickness of their sections in a plane model), so penalty has the meaning of an interface stiffness modulus per unit area, and the contact response converges under slave surface refinement. An axisymmetric model is refused: use surfacetodeformablesurfacepenalty with contactPoints=nodes there.

This constraint is a MeshDependent: if either surface’s source solid elements are refined mid-run (e.g. by hadaptivity), it regenerates that side’s facets from the current (refined) *surface definition and rebinds its cached facet list/reference coordinates at its own next updateConnectivity() tick – no separate wiring needed. A retained slave node (same across the rebuild, by Node identity) keeps its frictional history and augmented-Lagrange multiplier; a newly exposed one starts at rest.

surfacetodeformablesurfacepenalty - Integrated surface-to-surface contact

The integrated (Gauss-point-to-segment) counterpart of the constraint above: contact is evaluated at quadrature points over the slave surface’s facets, and the resulting pressure is distributed with each side’s parent element face shape functions rather than with the flat facets’ own.

Use this instead of nodetodeformablesurfacepenalty on quadratic (hexa20/quad8) contact surfaces. There, a node-based penalty scheme cannot transmit a correct pressure at all – the consistent nodal load of a uniform pressure at a serendipity corner is tensile, which a unilateral spring cannot exert, so the discrete solution opens every corner gap instead. The mechanism, the measurements, and why no choice of nodal weights can repair it are documented in Measured: the discrete solution lifts the corners off instead; the integrated formulation and its own limits are in Integrated contact: distributing with the parent face’s basis. On matched linear faces the parent-face basis coincides with the facet basis, so the two constraints agree to 13 digits and there is nothing to gain – but on non-matching meshes the integrated formulation is a substantial improvement at any element order, linear included, as the figure in Integrated contact: distributing with the parent face’s basis shows.

The constraint is one contact for every kind of contact point (One contact for every kind of contact point): the quadrature points of the slave facets (contactPoints=quadraturePoints, the default) and the slave nodes, each standing for its tributary area (contactPoints=nodes). Points that approach the master within searchDistance are paired with it; a point found inside without having been seen approaching gets no force, is reported, and counts as found inside until it is seen outside again. A point in contact slides from facet to facet, but not across a convex crease; in every update it walks over the master until its facet no longer changes, so that an update repeated at the same configuration, as a resumed run repeats one, pairs it the same. The master may have corners: at its creases – a crest, a re-entrant root – the gap is the distance to the crease, signed by its pseudonormal (Corners of the master: creases, the signed distance and the pseudonormal). A new mesh of the master keeps what the pairing learnt about the points; regenerated contact points are paired as at the first update. An overlap at the start within searchDistance (a deeper one is found inside, and reported) is either an interference fit, pushed apart at once or over initialOverlapRampTime (initialOverlap=pushApart, the default), or an error of the geometry, such as flat faces in a curved hole, kept as the stress-free state (initialOverlap=adjust; An initial overlap).

With type=tied the constraint ties its points to the master surface instead (A tie: contact that never opens and never slides). Mind the two type=: here it is the law of surfaceToDeformableSurfacePenalty (linear, quadratic or tied), while *constraint, type=tie is a different constraint, the tie by elimination below. With type=tied, each point within searchDistance at the first update is paired once, with the closest point of its closest facet, mapped onto the facet’s parent face, and held there in every direction, in tension and compression, by penalty springs; a gap or an overlap at the pairing is part of the state at rest. It joins independently meshed bodies in implicit and explicit analyses. A new mesh of either side hands the tie on: the same points stay tied to the same material points of the master (on a curved master, to the nearest points of the new chords, mapped onto the parent face; the resting offset takes up the difference, so the forces stay exact), displaced from rest as at the last accepted state (checkpointed), interpolated onto the new points of a refined slave side. It is not the tie constraint below, which eliminates the degrees of freedom of slave nodes exactly; the penalty tie is compliant by \(t/k\), adds to the frequencies of an explicit analysis, works for both kinds of contact point on the parent faces, and does not compete with hanging nodes. Both keep a gap at rest, the tie with adjust=False. Which to use when: Two ties.

Scope: frictionless normal penalty contact, or a tie, under sliding=small, on both the implicit and the explicit solver path, in 3D, plane and axisymmetric models. Coulomb friction and augmented Lagrange are not implemented here (the node-based constraint has both), and sliding=finite is rejected rather than approximated. The master surface must consist of a single element type, and so must a slave surface whose contact points are its quadrature points.

In 2D every contact point stands for its length times the out-of-plane extent of the body, as the elements integrate (Plane and axisymmetric models: one rule for the area of a point): in a plane model times the thickness of the section of its element, in an axisymmetric model (*job, domain=axisymmetric) for the ring it sweeps about the axis, \(2 \pi r\) times its length (Axisymmetric models: every point stands for a ring). penalty stays a modulus per unit area, and the pressures and tractions are per that area in the reference configuration. The job’s domain must match the elements – axisymmetric elements in an axisymmetric job, plane ones in a 2d job –, which nothing checks.

Module edelweissfe.constraints.surfacetodeformablesurfacepenalty

A penalty based unilateral contact constraint between the contact points of a slave body and a deformable master surface, whose force is distributed with the parent element face shape functions of the master – for every kind of contact point: the quadrature points of the faces of finite elements (a Gauss-point-to-segment, GPTS, formulation) and the nodes of finite elements. What a kind of point contributes – how it moves, which nodes its force goes to, the area it stands for – is its ContactPoints; everything else is written once, here.

Why the quadrature points exist alongside nodetodeformablesurfacepenalty: a node-based penalty scheme cannot transmit a correct pressure across a serendipity (quad8/hexa20) face, and no choice of nodal weights can repair it. The consistent nodal loads of a uniform pressure \(p\) on a quad8 face of area \(A\) are \(-pA/12\) at the corners and \(+pA/3\) at the midsides – the corner load is tensile, while a unilateral spring can only push. The discrete solution resolves that contradiction by opening the corner gaps: on a matched hexa20 patch test all corner nodes lift off, carrying no load, and the transmitted force falls ~1.7% short with a visibly undulating interface. See issue #112.

Integrating instead removes the contradiction at its root. The pressure is evaluated pointwise at quadrature points, where it is non-negative as physics requires, and is then distributed with the parent face’s own shape functions, so the nodal force at node \(a\),

\[f_a = \sum_q w_q \, J_q \, p_q \, N_a(\xi_q) ,\]

is negative wherever \(N_a\) is – a tensile nodal force arising from a non-negative pressure field, with no unilateral condition ever imposed at a corner node. Note that quadrature alone would achieve nothing: the flat facets’ own linear shape functions are non-negative, so distributing with those would leave the corners lifting off exactly as before. The parent-face basis (parentfacegeometry) is the load-bearing half of the formulation.

Scope of this implementation: frictionless normal penalty contact under sliding=small, for both the implicit and the explicit solver path. The springs come from an exchangeable law (penaltylaw), which owns what differs between laws – its springs, and whether it pairs the points once or anew in every search; the constraint owns everything the laws share. Coulomb friction and augmented Lagrange are not implemented (the node-based constraint has both); friction enters as a law with a per-point history (the module documentation of penaltylaw says what it implements). With the law type=tied the same constraint ties its points to the master surface instead, in every direction, with the gap or overlap at the pairing as the state at rest – e.g. two independently meshed bodies, see A tie: contact that never opens and never slides. sliding=finite is rejected rather than approximated, see Constraint.

What this formulation does not fix: pointwise enforcement at quadrature points still over-constrains a non-matching interface, and the integrand is discontinuous inside a slave facet wherever the assigned master facet changes, so that quadrature error does not vanish under refinement of the rule. Exactness on non-matching meshes requires segment clipping and weak enforcement through a multiplier field – mortar. This is the strictly weaker, strictly simpler member of that family, and it is what the corner-liftoff defect actually calls for.

surfacetodiscreterigidbodypenalty - Integrated contact against a discrete rigid body

The rigid-body counterpart of surfacetodeformablesurfacepenalty: contact is evaluated at the contact points of a slave surface and distributed with their interpolation vectors – by default at quadrature points, with the slave parent-face shape functions –, against the triangulated, closed surface of a discrete rigid body. The theory is documented in Integrated contact against a discrete rigid body. Use it for any contact against rigid supports or indenters, in particular with quadratic slave faces.

Its slave side is the same as that of surfacetodeformablesurfacepenalty (Contact points: the slave side as points): the quadrature points of the slave facets (contactPoints=quadraturePoints, the default) and the slave nodes, each standing for its tributary area (contactPoints=nodes) – so the nodes and the faces of finite elements hit a rigid body through one constraint. Points farther than searchDistance from the rigid surface take no part until they approach.

Scope: frictionless normal penalty contact under sliding=small, 3D, implicit and explicit solver paths, live AMR of the slave side. The slave surface must consist of a single element type.

Moving from nodeToDiscreteRigidBodyPenalty. That node-based constraint is removed; this one with contactPoints=nodes takes the same nodes as contact points. An input moves in four steps:

  1. The slave side is a surface, not a node set: generate the facets of the face that touches the body with the surfaceElementGenerator and give them as slaveSurface (nSet=block_back becomes the facets of surface=block_back), with contactPoints=nodes. On quadratic (hexa20/quad8) faces, take the default quadrature points instead: nodes cannot carry the tensile corner loads there (Measured: the discrete solution lifts the corners off instead).

  2. penalty is a stiffness per unit area: divide the penalty per node by the tributary area of a node, on a uniform mesh of linear faces of size \(h\) by \(h^2\). A node on the edge of the surface then has half the stiffness of an interior one, at a corner a quarter – its area – where the penalty per node gave every node the same; and the stiffness of the interface no longer changes with the mesh (Integrated contact against a discrete rigid body).

  3. The gap is that of small sliding: at every contact search (every increment of an implicit analysis) a node is paired with its closest triangle, and until the next search it sees that triangle’s plane, moving with the body. The removed constraint searched the closest point of the surface anew in every iteration (finite sliding). A node that slides over much of a triangle within one increment, past an edge or around a curved body, is therefore followed only from one increment to the next: take increments that are small against the triangles. searchDistance keeps its meaning: a node farther from the body takes no part.

  4. The surface of the rigid body must be closed and consist of triangles, which is checked when the constraint is created: split the quadrilaterals of a surface mesh in two (for instance with PyVista’s triangulate()) and give the body the triangulated file.

tie - Surface-to-surface tie (DOF elimination)

An Abaqus-style tie constraint bonding a slave surface rigidly to a deformable master surface. Unlike the penalty- and Lagrange-multiplier-based constraints above – among them the penalty tie, surfaceToDeformableSurfacePenalty with type=tied, compared with this one in Two ties – the tie is enforced by master-slave DOF elimination (multi-point constraint condensation): each slave node’s displacement is constrained to the frozen facet-linear interpolation of its closest master facet, \(u_s = \sum_a N_a \, u_{m_a}\), and the slave DOFs are condensed out of the system of equations – exact enforcement, no penalty parameter, no additional DOFs, zero added stiffness. The same mechanism serves the implicit solvers (system matrix transformation \(\tilde{K} = T^T K \, T + I_s\), the identity \(I_s\) filling the structurally zero slave rows and columns, followed by the recovery \(\delta u_s \mathrel{+}= \sum_a N_a \, \delta u_{m_a}\) after each linear solve – symmetric whenever \(K\) is, and free of any scale of its own, so it equilibrates cleanly also for a consistent mass matrix; see MultiPointConstraintTransformation) and explicit dynamics (mass-conserving row-sum folding of slave masses and forces onto the masters, direct kinematic slaving – the critical time step is untouched).

The implicit solver’s useThreadedMPCCondensation option (Solvers, NIST) computes \(T^T K T + I_s\) in place, with an OpenMP-threaded kernel (MPCCondensation), instead of with SciPy’s single-threaded CSR routines, which build a second matrix: the system matrix is held once, not twice. For this, the CSR pattern of \(K\) gains, once per discrete system, the entries where a master collects the couplings of its slaves (condensedPatternFillIn()); the slave columns of \(K\) become stored zeros in turn. Only the rows a constraint touches are computed, each by one thread from the rows of \(K\) it combines, in a fixed order of summation, so the result does not depend on the number of threads. Explicit zeros are pruned from a copy according to pruneCondensedMatrixZeros. It is off by default.

On matching interface meshes, tying is exactly equivalent to merging the interface nodes: the patch test passes to machine precision, and both the implicit and the explicit (central difference) solutions reproduce the monolithic mesh identically. On non-matching meshes the constraint is still enforced exactly, but the classical node-to-surface limitation applies: the slave surface’s consistent nodal force pattern, redistributed by the facet-linear weights, does not match the master’s own consistent pattern, so a uniform stress state exhibits a bounded, refinement-convergent interface perturbation (quantified in testfiles/edelweiss-only/TieHexa20Patch/test_mismatched.inp) – the same obstruction that motivates mortar methods.

For quadratic (hexa20/quad8) faces, generate both facet surfaces with triangulation=midside: the corner triangulation excludes the midside nodes from the facet node lists entirely, leaving slave midside nodes untied.

Contested slave degrees of freedom

A degree of freedom can be condensed out only once, so when several multi-point constraints ask for the same slave DOF, exactly one may keep it. This arises routinely under \(h\)-adaptivity: a hanging node created on a refined tie interface is a hangingnode slave, and the tie would like to constrain it too.

The decision is made centrally, where every constraint’s records are collected, and not inside any constraint: the first constraint in model order keeps the DOF, and later claims on it are dropped and reported. Resolving it centrally is what makes the result independent of the order in which constraints are refreshed – a constraint that inspected its peers mid-refresh would read some of them in their pre-refinement state.

Model order therefore carries meaning. For the hanging-node case the precedence is not arbitrary, and it is expressed by hAdaptivity registering its hanging-node constraint at the front of the model’s multi-point constraints:

  • If the hanging-node constraint keeps the node, the refined and unrefined meshes stay exactly conforming, and the node is still tied – indirectly but almost exactly, because its coarse parent nodes are themselves tie slaves and it is their interpolation.

  • If the tie keeps it instead, the node is bonded exactly, but nothing holds it on its coarse parent edge any more, and the refined and unrefined meshes come apart there.

One constraint has a stand-in for its condition and the other does not, which is what settles it. Measured on the coarse anchor pry-out, the two precedences differ by 5.3e-02 relative displacement and eventually by the refined mesh itself.

Note

This used to be resolved inside the tie, which built its records against its peers’ claimedSlaveNodes() while the refresh sweep was still running – so peers that had not yet refreshed answered with their pre-refinement claims. A node that had been a hanging node before the latest refinement, and was not one after it, was reported as still claimed, and the tie dropped it, leaving it constrained by nothing at all.

Multi-point constraints and Dirichlet boundary conditions

A degree of freedom eliminated by a multi-point constraint – a tie slave, a hangingnode slave – cannot also be prescribed by a Dirichlet boundary condition: the constraint already determines it from its masters, and a prescribed value would be a second, independent equation for the same unknown. Where a boundary condition’s node set overlaps a constraint’s slave nodes, the solvers resolve the overlap automatically: the conflicting constraint equation is dropped, for that component only, so that the boundary condition takes precedence. This is how Abaqus resolves the same overlap, and it needs no configuration.

The alternative – rejecting such a model and requiring the user to subtract the constraint’s slave nodes from the boundary condition’s node set – is worth understanding, because input files written for solvers that demand it often carry such a subtraction. It is a snapshot: computed against one mesh, one tie tolerance and one refinement state, and silently no longer correct when any of them changes, with no diagnostic, because what remains is a plain list of node labels that no longer records why those labels were chosen. It also interferes with \(h\)-adaptivity, which extends a node set to newly created nodes only where the whole parent face or edge already lies within the set: every face touching a subtracted node stops propagating the boundary condition to its children, so the boundary condition thins out with each refinement. Such subtractions should be removed rather than maintained.

Dropped equations are reported in two classes, and the distinction matters:

redundant

Every master carrying non-negligible weight is itself prescribed, and the weighted sum reproduces the value prescribed on the slave. The constraint equation and the boundary condition say the same thing, so dropping either changes nothing. Reported as a count, at journal level 1. This is the expected case for a symmetry plane cutting through a tied interface: the slave and its masters all lie on the plane and all carry the same prescribed value.

not implied

They do not say the same thing – some master is unprescribed, or the weighted sum disagrees. The boundary condition still wins, but the model has genuinely changed, so this is reported as a warning naming how much of the constraint weight rests on unprescribed masters. It is a signal worth chasing rather than silencing: the usual cause is a boundary condition whose node set is incomplete, so that masters which ought to carry the condition do not.

Masters are resolved transitively before classification – a master may itself be a slave of another constraint – and weights are compared against a scaled tolerance, never against exact zero, since clamped closest-point projections routinely leave weights at round-off magnitude.

Implementing your own constraints

Subclass from the constraint base class in module edelweissfe.constraints.base.constraintbase and implement its computeKernels. A constraint enters the discrete system like an element does (see The residual and the tangent): it adds its residual contribution \(\mathbf{P}\) – the negative of the force it exerts on its nodes – and the tangent \(\mathbf{K} = \partial \mathbf{P} / \partial \mathbf{U}\). Two examples:

  • A penalty spring of stiffness \(k\) that pulls a degree of freedom \(u\) towards \(\bar{u}\) exerts the force \(-k\,(u - \bar{u})\) on its node, so it adds \(P \mathrel{+}= k\,(u - \bar{u})\) and \(K \mathrel{+}= k\), exactly as a spring element would. directionalspringpenalty is this constraint.

  • A Lagrange multiplier \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) enforcing \(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{0}\) adds \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^T \partial \mathbf{g} / \partial \mathbf{u}\) to the rows of the nodes and \(\mathbf{g}\) to the rows of the multipliers, with the tangent \(\begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\lambda}^T \partial^2 \mathbf{g} / \partial \mathbf{u}^2 & (\partial \mathbf{g} / \partial \mathbf{u})^T \\ \partial \mathbf{g} / \partial \mathbf{u} & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix}\). equalvaluelagrangian and rigidbody are such constraints.

Because \(\mathbf{K}\) is the derivative of exactly what is added to \(\mathbf{P}\), a constraint can be checked by finite differences of its own computeKernels: a wrong tangent, or a wrong sign of \(\mathbf{P}\) alone, shows up as a mismatch. \(\mathbf{P}\) and \(\mathbf{K}\) negated together pass this check, so the test of a constraint also checks the direction of the forces it exerts on its nodes: a penalty pulls them back towards what it prescribes, a contact pushes the bodies apart, a Lagrange multiplier acts on them as \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^T \partial \mathbf{g} / \partial \mathbf{u}\) with the constraint’s own \(\mathbf{g}\). tests/test_residual_tangent_convention.py does both for the constraints shipped with EdelweissFE (the two integrated surface contact constraints in their own test modules); add a case there for a new one. The helpers it uses are part of the package, so that the tests of a package with constraints of its own use them as well:

Helpers for checking the rule every constraint follows: it adds a residual contribution P and the tangent K = dP/dU (see the solver documentation, “The residual and the tangent”).

Because K must be the derivative of what is added to P, comparing it with central differences of P checks both at once: a wrong tangent, or a P whose sign alone is wrong, shows up as a mismatch. A P and a K negated together pass, so a test checks the direction of the forces the constraint exerts as well, which only the mechanics of the constraint can say: that a penalty pulls its nodes back, that a contact pushes the bodies apart.

They are part of the package, so that the tests of a package that adds constraints of its own can check them the same way as the tests of EdelweissFE check its constraints.

A constraint that is not active gets no degrees of freedom, the solvers do not evaluate it, and its scalar variables – the Lagrange multipliers of equalvaluelagrangian, say – leave the discrete system with it, so that they leave no unknowns without an equation behind. Two states decide it, and active is true while both are:

A constraint reports from updateConnectivity only whether the nodes it couples changed. A constraint that searches as the bodies move, e.g. a contact, which searches for the surface it touches, declares it with connectivityChangesWithMotion = True, whether or not the nodes it couples change with the search (the contact with a discrete rigid body without searchDistance locates every point and keeps its nodes, but finds their closest triangles anew): it has to be updated again and again, so a solver that never updates the connectivity (NEST) refuses it, and NED updates it only every contact-update-frequency increments. So a constraint switched off from outside stays off, whatever it finds in reach; switched on again, it takes part while it is within reach. Switched off from outside, a constraint rests: the model does not locate it anew in updateConnectivity, which it does for every constraint that is switched on, those out of reach included, so that they can find themselves within reach again. A constraint that is not active accepts no state either (acceptLastState): a contact switched off for a step keeps its multipliers and its frictional history as they were. A solver that cannot handle a constraint, e.g. NEST a contact, refuses it only while it is switched on.

Penalty springs, and the bound of their stiffness that the explicit solver adds to the squared frequencies of the degrees of freedom they couple, which lowers the critical time step.

A penalty constraint acts through springs. A spring \(p\) measures a stretch \(g_p\) – the gap of a contact point, the displacement of a tied point from rest or of a held point from its prescribed value along one axis –; the vector with which it acts on the degrees of freedom of the constraint is the gradient of the stretch, \(\boldsymbol{a}_p = \partial g_p/\partial \boldsymbol{u}\), and its law gives it a force \(f_p\) and a stiffness \(k_p = \mathrm{d}f_p/\mathrm{d}g_p\). The residual contribution of the springs is \(\sum_p f_p \boldsymbol{a}_p\), their stiffness \(\sum_p k_p\, \boldsymbol{a}_p \otimes \boldsymbol{a}_p\).

The explicit dynamic solver NED needs the row sums of the absolute value of that stiffness, weighted by column (addBoundOfAbsoluteStiffnessTimes()). The absolute value of a sum is at most the sum of the absolute values, so, with the weights \(\boldsymbol{w}\) of the columns,

\[b_i \mathrel{+}= \sum_p |k_p|\, |a_{p,i}| \, \big(|\boldsymbol{a}_p| \cdot \boldsymbol{w}\big) :\]

one product of the absolute gradients with the weights per spring, and one distribution back, as the force is distributed. The stiffness of a spring is symmetric, so this bounds the rows of its symmetric part as well, as the contract asks. addBoundOfAbsoluteStiffnessOfSprings() is that formula, written once for every penalty constraint of EdelweissFE and EdelweissMeshfree that bounds its springs instead of its tangent: one that has no tangent, or whose tangent is costly, overrides addBoundOfAbsoluteStiffnessTimes with it. A constraint takes its springs for the bound from the same code it takes them for its force, so that the force and the bound cannot drift apart. A contact law gives its springs at the paired contact points as PenaltySprings (penaltylaw).

The gradient of a spring moves nodes along the direction of the spring, \(a_{p,(n,j)} = C_{pn}\, d_{pj}\): the coefficient \(C_{pn}\) with which the spring moves node \(n\) (an interpolation vector, or the negative shape functions of a master face) times the component \(j\) of its direction. It comes in parts, of two kinds:

  • SpringGradientOnSharedNodes: the springs share their nodes, and the coefficients are a matrix over them – the interpolation matrix of contact points (interpolationMatrixOfLocatedPoints()), the interpolation vectors of the points of a particle;

  • SpringGradientOnOwnNodes: every spring has nodes of its own in the block – the parent face nodes of the master facet of a contact point, a slave node and its facet, the reference point of a rigid body.

Over the next increment. With follow-critical-time-step, NED asks for the bound over the states the next increment can reach (NextIncrement). A spring that switches on where its stretch turns negative – a contact point that closes – is in that bound if it is closed at its smallest stretch within the increment. With the pairing and the normals held, as within an increment, the gap of a point is affine in the solution, and so its smallest value within the increment is at a corner of the states the increment can reach, \(g + \min(0, \Delta g_v) + \min(0, \Delta g_a)\), with the changes of the gap by the part of the increment from the velocity and by the one from the acceleration (smallestGapsWithin()). A constraint that bounds its springs takes them at these gaps, and sums over its springs: a point that could close within the increment has its spring in the bound before it closes, whichever degrees of freedom the springs share, and a law whose stiffness grows with the depth is stiffest there.

The penalty contacts are assembled from two pieces of their own: the contact points of the slave side (Contact points: the slave side as points) and the law that puts penalty springs at the paired points (The law: what differs, and the place of friction). A contact constraint takes its slave side as contact points: points that move with an interpolation vector over the nodes they act on and stand for an area. The interface says what a constraint needs from them; the two kinds are the nodes of a slave surface and the quadrature points of its faces. A new kind of contact point implements ContactPoints:

The contact points of a contact constraint: the points of the slave side at which contact is enforced.

A contact constraint pairs points of a slave body with a master surface, measures the gap of each point along the master’s normal, and turns the gap into a force (see penaltylaw). How the points move, which nodes their force goes to, and the area each of them stands for depends on the kind of point, and on nothing else:

  • a node of a finite element: \(c = [1]\), at \(\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{X} + \boldsymbol{U}\);

  • a quadrature point \(\xi_q\) of a finite element face: \(c = N^s(\xi_q)\), the shape functions of the face, at \(\boldsymbol{x} = N^s(\xi_q) \cdot (\boldsymbol{X}^s + \boldsymbol{U}^s)\).

A point moves with its interpolation vector \(c\) and its force is distributed to the nodes with the same vector, the transpose of its motion: then the penalty spring at the point has a symmetric stiffness and does no work over a closed cycle.

A point’s position is told in two parts: its origin \(\boldsymbol{x}^0\), where it is when the displacement it moves with is zero, and its displacement \(c \cdot \boldsymbol{u}\) from there. For the points of finite elements, \(\boldsymbol{u}\) is U, the total displacement of their nodes, and the origin is the point in the reference configuration. A kind of point whose nodes carry the increment rather than the total displacement fits the same interface: for it, \(\boldsymbol{u}\) is dU, the increment, and the origin the point at the start of the increment. EdelweissMeshfree adds such a kind, the points on the faces of its updated-Lagrangian particles (ContactPointsOnParticleFaces). A constraint therefore passes both, and each kind takes the one it moves with. The split also lets a constraint that measures a small distance between two points – the stretch of a stiff penalty spring – take the difference of the origins once and the difference of the displacements in every evaluation, rather than the difference of the coordinates, whose round-off scales with the coordinates (see Measured from the origins, not from the coordinates).

Every contact point stands for an area \(A_p\), its share of the surface of the body: the penalty per unit area times it is the stiffness of its spring. The area is the one the elements integrate over. They integrate over the body with \(\mathrm{d}V = b\, \mathrm{d}A\), with the out-of-plane extent \(b\) of the body at a point (outOfPlaneExtentAt()): \(b = 1\) in a 3D model, the thickness \(t\) of the plane section in a plane model, the circumference \(2 \pi r\) at the radius \(r\) in an axisymmetric model, the section r-z of a body of revolution. So the surface of the body is \(\mathrm{d}S = b\, \mathrm{d}L\), and a contact point stands for its measure \(L_p\) in the model’s own space – an area in 3D, a length in 2D: its quadrature weight times the Jacobian of its face, or the tributary measure of a node – times \(b\) at the point,

\[A_p = b(\boldsymbol{X}_p) \, L_p .\]

A node sums this over its faces, \(A_s = \sum_f b_f(\boldsymbol{X}_s)\, s_{s,f}\) with its shares \(s_{s,f}\) of their measures: a node between faces of two thicknesses stands for its share of the area of each. The springs then act on the same body as the elements – in an axisymmetric model on the full rings – and the two balance at the nodes. A point on the axis of an axisymmetric model stands for a ring of radius 0, no area: the axis is a line, not a surface of the body. The pressure and the traction of a point are its force per \(A_p\), the area in the reference configuration: the nominal pressure, not per radian, not per unit thickness. The axial forces of the points of an axisymmetric model add up to the total axial force; the radial forces of a ring cancel about the axis, so a sum of radial components is no resultant.

Every kind of point measures its areas when its points are made. The two kinds of finite element points take the model and measure with outOfPlaneExtentAt(). A kind of point of another library measures them as its own bodies integrate – in an axisymmetric model with circumferenceAt(), the same rule of the axis –, and says with ContactPoints.axisymmetric whether it was made for an axisymmetric model; a constraint refuses points made for a model of another kind (refusePointsMadeForAnotherKindOfModel()). Whether the bodies of such a kind are slices of the same thickness as the finite elements they meet in a plane model is not checked: that is for the model to ensure.

ContactPoints is what a constraint needs from its contact points; a constraint written against it – the pairing with the master, the gap, the law – is written once for every kind of point. Two kinds are here: the nodes of a slave surface (contactpointsatslavenodes) and the quadrature points of its faces (contactpointsonslavesurface). class edelweissfe.constraints.base.contactpoints.ContactPoints[source]

The contact points of the slave side of a contact constraint.

A constraint uses them so. In every update of the connectivity, it asks for the current coordinates of all points (currentPointCoordinates()), pairs those within reach with the master surface, and then locates the paired ones (locatePoints()): they name the nodes they act on, which become the slave part of the constraint’s block. class edelweissfe.constraints.base.contactpointsonslavesurface.ContactPointsOnSlaveSurface(slaveSurface, nQuadraturePoints, model, constraintName)[source]

The quadrature points of a slave surface, used as contact points.

Each slave facet carries an nQuadraturePoints-point rule. A point at facet-barycentric coordinates \(b\) sits at parent-face parametric coordinates \(\xi = b \cdot \Xi\) (with \(\Xi\) the facet vertices’ own parametric coordinates in the parent face, stamped by the surface element generator), and carries the constant weight \(L_p = w_q J_q\), the quadrature weight times the reference measure \(J_q\) of the facet. Its position follows the curved parent surface, \(x_q = N^s(\xi_q) \cdot x^s\). The area it stands for is \(L_p\) times the out-of-plane extent of the body at its origin (outOfPlaneExtentAt()), measured from the model when the points are made: times the thickness of the section of its facet’s element in a plane model, \(2 \pi r_q L_p\), the area of its ring, in an axisymmetric model.

The contact points are numbered by slave facet and, within a facet, by quadrature point. The faces of the slave surface must be of one type (one number of parent face nodes), so that the located points are evaluated as one array. A located point takes the nodes of its parent face into the block of the constraint, its own copy of them: its interpolation vector is \(N^s(\xi_q)\) over them, and it moves with their total displacement, from its origin in the reference configuration, \(N^s(\xi_q) \cdot X^s\).

A new law derives from PenaltyLaw (its springs, and whether it pairs once), a new normal contact law from ContactPenaltyLaw (its normal force and energy):

The laws of the penalty springs of the contact constraints: which springs a contact point gets, and how hard they pull.

A contact constraint is made of three pieces: its contact points (where the slave surface is, see contactpoints), the geometry that pairs each of them with a point of the master surface and measures where it is relative to that point, and the law that turns this into penalty springs (PenaltySprings). The law is a separate object, held by the constraint. It owns what differs from one law to the next, and nothing else:

  • its springs (PenaltyLaw.springs()): given, for the paired points, their gaps along the frozen normals, the normals, the penalty times the area of each point and the displacement of each point from where it rests, the springs the law puts there – their points, directions, forces, stiffnesses and stored energies;

  • how it pairs (PenaltyLaw.pairsOnce): anew in every search of the connectivity, or once, for good.

The constraint owns what every law shares: the contact points and where they are, the search and the pairing, the relative position and the gap, the force and the tangent of the springs on the nodes, the bound of their stiffness for the critical time step (penaltysprings), the state of the points in a checkpoint, and the outputs, which it derives from the springs alone. So a new law takes its place without touching the constraint.

Two kinds of law exist:

  • ContactPenaltyLaw: contact, a unilateral normal spring at every point whose gap is closed. Its normal force follows from the gap alone (ContactPenaltyLaw.normalForce()), which is also what the node-based contacts (one gap per slave node) ask of it. LinearPenaltyLaw and QuadraticPenaltyLaw.

  • TiedPenaltyLaw: a tie, paired once and held in every direction by a linear spring along every axis.

A friction law (not implemented) would be a PenaltyLaw that pairs anew and adds tangential springs whose resting offsets, the stick points, are its history; its design is in the theory, The law: what differs, and the place of friction.

Sign convention: the gap \(g\) is negative when the surfaces penetrate, and the force \(f\) of a spring carries the sign of its stretch, i.e. \(f_n \le 0\) in contact. The stiffness \(\mathrm{d}f/\mathrm{d}g\) is then positive for \(g < 0\). penaltyTimesArea is the penalty modulus times the area the contact point represents: a nodal tributary area in node-based formulations, a quadrature weight times the facet measure in integrated ones, each times the out-of-plane extent \(b\) (1 in 3D, the thickness of a plane model, \(2 \pi r\) in an axisymmetric one; Plane and axisymmetric models: one rule for the area of a point).

class edelweissfe.constraints.base.penaltylaw.TiedPenaltyLaw[source]

A tie: each point is paired once, and held where it rests by a linear spring along every axis, in tension and in compression.

The constraint pairs each point once, and the vector from its master point to it at the pairing is where it rests (A tie: contact that never opens and never slides). Along every axis \(\boldsymbol{e}_i\), the stretch of the spring is the displacement of the point from rest in that direction, \(d_i\), its force \(f_i = k A\, d_i\), its stiffness \(k A\), its energy \(\tfrac{1}{2} k A\, d_i^2\).
